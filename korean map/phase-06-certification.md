# PHASE 6 — PRECISION AND CERTIFICATION

**Korean Roadmap · Levels 15–16 of 18**

> **Phase goal.** Two things happen here at once. First, you take the language you already have and make it *measurable* — an externally judged score you can put on a document. Second, you learn a craft that forces you to understand Korean more deeply than fluency ever does: translation.
>
> **The shift.** Everything before this was about *using* Korean. This phase is about *accountability*. A tutor can be generous. An examiner is not. A client is not.
>
> **Why these two belong together.** TOPIK 6 is roughly the level at which professional translation into Korean becomes possible. And translation is the single fastest way to find the exact holes in your grammar that a score of 230 will expose but never explain. The exam tells you that you have a gap. Translation tells you where it is.

# LEVEL 15 — TOPIK II MASTERY

> **Goal.** Score 230 or above on a full, timed, cold TOPIK II paper. Not 230 on a practice set you have seen before, and not 230 assembled from your best section scores on different days — 230 in one sitting, under exam conditions, with the clock running.

## Level dashboard

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Level** | 15 — TOPIK II Mastery |
| **Difficulty** | Advanced |
| **Suggested duration** | 8–12 weeks |
| **Study commitment** | 2–3 hours per day, one full mock every two weeks |
| **Core tools** | Official past papers (at least 10 sets), an answer sheet, a stopwatch, a marker who scores 쓰기 |
| **Main project** | **`topik-kit`** — three full timed mocks, a writing portfolio, and an error ledger |
| **Final outcome** | ≥ 230/300 on a paper you have never seen, with 쓰기 ≥ 72. |

**Prerequisites.** Level 14 complete. In practice this means: you can read a newspaper editorial without a dictionary (L14), you know 300 Hanja at recall (L11), and you have written 1,200 words of 해라체 argument that a native did not correct for grammar (L12).

## 15.1 — What the exam actually measures

**Objective.** Understand the test as a designed instrument before you try to beat it.

### Module 15.1.1 — The three sections

| Section | Questions | Time | Points | What it really tests |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **듣기** Listening | 50 | 60 min | 100 | Comprehension at native speed, once, with no replay |
| **쓰기** Writing | 4 | 50 min | 100 | Controlled production under severe time pressure |
| **읽기** Reading | 50 | 70 min | 100 | Speed-reading and inference, not careful reading |

**Total: 300 points, 180 minutes, one sitting, one morning.**

### Module 15.1.2 — The score bands

| Band | Score | What it certifies |
|---|---|---|
| TOPIK 3 | 120–149 | Can function in daily life; cannot work in Korean |
| TOPIK 4 | 150–189 | Can hold a job with support; reads simple work documents |
| TOPIK 5 | 190–229 | Can work independently; reads newspapers with effort |
| **TOPIK 6** | **230–300** | Can work at professional level; reads academic and literary text |

**Your target is 230. That is 77% — not 90%.** This single fact changes strategy completely. You are not trying to be excellent. You are trying to be *unbreakable in the parts you control*.

### Module 15.1.3 — Where the 230 comes from

The most useful thing you can do in your first week is decide, in advance and on paper, which questions you intend to get right.

| Section | Questions you must secure | Points from those | Remaining margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 듣기 | Q1–35 with high confidence, 10 of Q36–50 | ≈ 90 | 10 |
| 읽기 | Q1–40 with high confidence, 5 of Q41–50 | ≈ 90 | 10 |
| 쓰기 | Q51, Q52, Q53 near-perfect, Q54 at 60% | ≈ 72 | — |
| **Total** | | **≈ 252** | ample |

> **The arithmetic that decides your life for 180 minutes:** you can leave the last ten questions of 읽기 blank and still score 6, provided 쓰기 holds. You cannot leave 쓰기 to chance, because the section is only four questions — one bad essay costs you 20 points, which is ten reading questions' worth of margin.

### Module 15.1.4 — Know the exam day

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 09:20 | Doors close. Late arrivals are refused — no exceptions, no appeals |
| 09:30–10:30 | 듣기 |
| 10:30–10:35 | Short break |
| 10:35–11:25 | 쓰기 |
| 11:25–11:35 | Break |
| 11:35–12:45 | 읽기 |

**Things that are not obvious until they cost you:** you must write your answers on the OMR sheet as you go, because there is no separate transfer time at the end. Bring a correction tape, not correction fluid — fluid is banned in most centres. Your ID must be the exact one you registered with.

### Practice tasks

1. Download three official past papers. Do not open them yet.
2. Sit one paper cold, untimed, and score it honestly. Record the section subtotals. This is your baseline, and it will be worse than you expect — that is the point.
3. Write your intended question-by-question strategy on one page, in pencil, and revise it after every mock.

### Module completion gate

- You have a written baseline score for all three sections.
- You can state, without looking, the point value of a single reading question and the total margin between 190 and 230.
- You have read the official candidate rules for your test centre.

## 15.2 — 듣기: the anatomy of the section

**Objective.** Stop listening to everything. The listening section is 50 questions with a designed difficulty ramp, and each block tests a different micro-skill.

### Module 15.2.1 — The question blocks

| Block | Questions | The task | Why it is hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 1–3 | Choose the matching picture or graphic | vocabulary of everyday objects and places |
| **B** | 4–8 | Choose the correct response (이어지는 말) | the polite-but-wrong answer is always present |
| **C** | 9–12 | Identify where the conversation is happening / what follows | requires inference from register and vocabulary |
| **D** | 13–16 | Identify the man's or woman's central point | the other speaker says the plausible wrong answer |
| **E** | 17–20 | Identify the topic | the opening sentence is a decoy |
| **F** | 21–30 | Detail, number, time, reason | numbers are said once and often corrected |
| **G** | 31–40 | Longer dialogues: attitude, intention, relationship | speakers contradict themselves |
| **H** | 41–50 | Monologues: lectures, documentaries, news analysis | 60–90 seconds of continuous native speech per item |

### Module 15.2.2 — What you are allowed to read

You get the answer options **before** the audio starts, and there is a short gap. That gap is the whole exam.

**The 30-second pre-read.** Read the four options, not to understand them but to build a prediction list:

| Option type | What you extract before the audio |
|---|---|
| Four nouns/phrases | the topic field — one of these four will be said |
| Four verbs of attitude | `찬성한다` `반대한다` `우려한다` `기대한다` → listen for stance markers, not content |
| Four numbers | write the four numbers down; the audio will say all four, one of them twice |
| Four locations | listen for the particle `에서` and the place-vocabulary field |

### Module 15.2.3 — The three distractors the exam always uses

Every wrong answer in block D–H is one of these. Learn to recognise them and the section gets dramatically easier.

| Distractor | How it works | How to catch it |
|---|---|---|
| **The plausible paraphrase** | an option that restates the *other* speaker's position accurately | check *whose* idea the question asks about — usually one named sex |
| **The true-but-irrelevant** | a statement that is factually in the audio but is not the answer to *this* question | read the question stem first, not the options |
| **The overreach** | an option that is true but goes one step too far — `항상` `절대` `모든` `반드시` | extreme quantifiers are almost never correct |

> **The single most reliable listening rule:** if an option contains `항상`, `절대`, `모두`, or `반드시`, it is wrong unless the speaker literally used that word. The exam writers use absolutes as a trap because they are easy to construct and easy to detect.

### Practice tasks

1. Take one past paper's 듣기 and do the first twenty questions with the transcript visible. Mark every place where the correct answer was paraphrased rather than repeated. You will find that almost none are verbatim.
2. Do the same twenty questions again with audio only. Compare.
3. Build a list of fifty attitude verbs and their typical audio cues (`걱정스럽다` → `-ㄹ까 봐`, `아쉽다` → `-았/었으면`, `기대된다` → `-ㄹ 것 같다`).

### Module completion gate

- You can name the eight blocks and what each one tests.
- You score ≥ 85/100 on a 듣기 section you have not heard before.
- You have caught at least five "overreach" options in a single paper and can point to the word that gave them away.

## 15.3 — 듣기: the tactics that survive the audio

**Objective.** Build the reflex set that carries you through blocks G and H, where the audio outpaces your ability to think.

### Module 15.3.1 — The four-second rule

When you miss something, you get **four seconds** to decide: keep listening or abandon the question. Most candidates lose five questions' worth of points by still processing question 34 while question 35 is playing.

**The decision rule:** if you cannot reconstruct the answer from your notes within four seconds of the audio ending, mark a guess and move. A guess is worth 0 or 2. A lost question 35 because you were still on 34 is also 0 or 2 — but you have now lost the option of guessing well.

### Module 15.3.2 — Note-taking that fits on one line

You are not transcribing. You are writing a skeleton.

| Symbol | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `↑` `↓` | increase / decrease | `매출 ↑ 12%` |
| `→` | therefore, leads to | `규제 → 비용 ↑` |
| `≠` | contrast, however | `계획 ≠ 실제` |
| `:` | because / reason | `취소 : 예산` |
| `?` | uncertainty, hedging | `? 내년` |

**Rule:** one line per question, maximum four words. If you are writing a sentence, you are not listening.

### Module 15.3.3 — The stance vocabulary

Blocks G and H ask `남자의 태도는?` or `여자의 중심 생각은?` far more often than they ask for facts. The answer is almost always carried by one of these markers, not by the content.

| Korean cue | The stance it signals |
|---|---|
| `-기는 하지만` | reluctant concession → the real point is after it |
| `-다 보니` | unintended consequence → usually the cause they admit |
| `-ㄹ 수밖에 없다` | resignation, not enthusiasm |
| `-지 않을까 싶다` | hedged opinion → the speaker's actual view |
| `그건 좀` + pause | disagreement, softened |
| `솔직히 말하면` | the speaker is about to contradict something said earlier |
| `글쎄요` | refusal to commit → answer is usually "uncertain" |

### Module 15.3.4 — Numbers, times, and the correction reflex

Korean exam audio says a number and then corrects it roughly a third of the time: `세 시... 아, 세 시 반이죠.` If you write the first number down and stop listening, you lose the question.

**The rule:** when you hear a number, hold your pencil above the paper. Write only after the next clause finishes.

### Practice tasks

1. Transcribe five block-H monologues in full, then compare against the official transcript. Count your errors by type: vocabulary, grammar, speed, or attention.
2. Do a full 듣기 section at 1.0× speed, then again at 1.15×. The second run is harder than the exam — it is training, not simulation.
3. Keep a "missed because" tally for every mock: unknown word / too fast / lost my place / misread the question.

### Module completion gate

- Fewer than three "lost my place" errors across a full section.
- You can identify speaker stance from the marker alone, with the content removed, on ten items.
- 듣기 ≥ 85/100 on two consecutive unseen papers.

## 15.4 — 읽기: the clock is the opponent

**Objective.** Understand that 읽기 is not a reading test. It is a *rate* test with reading questions attached.

### Module 15.4.1 — The arithmetic of failure

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Questions | 50 |
| Minutes | 70 |
| Minutes per question | **1.4** |
| Seconds per question | **84** |

Eighty-four seconds. A Korean newspaper paragraph is roughly 45 seconds of careful reading for a competent reader — and the later passages are longer, denser, and often technical.

**The consequence:** the passages in blocks A–E must be read in 40–50 seconds, because block H passages need 3–4 minutes each. If you spend 3 minutes on question 12, you have already failed question 45.

### Module 15.4.2 — The question blocks

| Block | Questions | The task | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 1–2 | Choose the grammatically correct sentence | three options are colloquially plausible but formally wrong |
| **B** | 3–4 | Insert the given sentence | the correct slot is often *not* where the topic seems to shift |
| **C** | 5–8 | Match content to passage | one option is true but not in this passage |
| **D** | 9–12 | Identify the topic | the topic is the noun that repeats, not the first noun |
| **E** | 13–15 | Order the sentences | the first sentence is usually not the one that starts with a connector |
| **F** | 16–18 | Insert a sentence into a paragraph | connectors `그러나` `따라서` `예를 들어` mark the seam |
| **G** | 19–20 | Choose the grammar form for the blank | register decides it, not meaning |
| **H** | 21–27 | Content, title, purpose | three options paraphrase the same clause |
| **I** | 28–34 | Match a statement to a passage or a graph plus passage | the graph carries half the answer |
| **J** | 35–41 | Insert / infer / attitude | requires reading the whole passage once |
| **K** | 42–50 | Long academic and literary passages | 3–4 minutes each, and they are worth the same 2 points |

### Module 15.4.3 — The 84-second method

| Phase | Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Stem** | 8 s | Read the question and options first. You now know what to hunt for |
| **2. Scan** | 40 s | Read the passage *for the answer*, not for comprehension. Headings, connectors, and the sentence containing the keyword |
| **3. Decide** | 20 s | Eliminate the overreach and the true-but-irrelevant. If two remain, choose the one that matches the passage's *scope* |
| **4. Mark** | 10 s | Fill the OMR. Never defer — you will not come back |

### Module 15.4.4 — When to guess and move

**Hard rule for block K:** if you reach question 42 with fewer than 20 minutes remaining, you answer the remaining questions by keyword matching only — read the question stem, find the keyword's sentence, read that sentence and one before it, answer. You will get about half. That is 4–5 points you would otherwise lose to blank answers.

### Practice tasks

1. Do a full 읽기 section with a visible stopwatch, and write the timestamp when you finish each question. You are looking for the questions that ate 4 minutes.
2. Do blocks A–G only, 27 questions, in 25 minutes. Repeat until you finish with two minutes spare.
3. Read one block-K passage from a past paper with no time limit, then read it again with a 3-minute limit. Note what you had to give up.

### Module completion gate

- You finish all 50 questions in 70 minutes on three consecutive attempts, guessing no more than six.
- 읽기 ≥ 80/100 on an unseen paper.
- You can name, from memory, the four-step method and the block-K fallback.

## 15.5 — 읽기: the question-type playbook

**Objective.** Turn the eleven blocks into half a dozen repeatable procedures.

### Module 15.5.1 — Insert-the-sentence (blocks B, F)

The single most learnable question type on the paper. The inserted sentence must attach to *something* — a pronoun, a repeated noun, or a connector.

| Diagnostic | What to look for in the sentence being inserted |
|---|---|
| `이`, `그`, `저` + noun | there must be an antecedent immediately before the slot |
| `이러한`, `그런` | a description must precede; a general claim must follow |
| `그러나`, `하지만` | the previous sentence must contain the opposite |
| `따라서`, `그러므로` | the previous sentence must contain the cause |
| `예를 들어` | the previous sentence must contain the general claim being exemplified |
| no connector, no pronoun | the slot is where the paragraph's *topic* changes — usually last |

### Module 15.5.2 — Order-the-sentences (block E)

Do not read them in order. Find the sentence that **cannot** come first — the one that opens with a connector or a pronoun — and build outward from it.

Method:
1. Cross out every sentence beginning with a connector. Those are not first.
2. Find the sentence with a bare noun subject and no back-reference. That is usually first.
3. Chain by pronoun and by `-는데` / `-지만` / `-어서`.
4. Check the last sentence ends the paragraph's argument, not merely a detail.

### Module 15.5.3 — The grammar-blank (block G)

These test register and collocation, not meaning. All four options fit semantically. The deciding factors:

| Factor | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Register** | is the passage 해요체, 합니다체, or 해라체? A `-습니다` form in a 해라체 passage is wrong regardless of meaning |
| **Collocation** | `-에 달려 있다`, `-을 수밖에 없다`, `-기 마련이다` take fixed partners |
| **Tense chain** | the blank must agree with the tense established two sentences earlier |
| **Speaker stance** | `-더라` implies hearsay or recollection; `-겠-` implies intention or supposition |

### Module 15.5.4 — The long academic passage (block K)

These come from real sources: economics, science, sociology, literary criticism. The vocabulary is beyond the 6,000-word list, and that is deliberate — the exam tests whether you can survive unknown words.

**The three-sentence strategy:** read sentence 1 (the claim), the sentence containing the keyword from the question, and the last sentence (the conclusion). Skip the middle. In most block-K passages, the middle is elaboration, and the same 2 points ride on the edges.

### Practice tasks

1. Take ten insert-the-sentence questions and, before answering, underline every deictic and connector. Answer using only the underlined words. Score yourself.
2. Take five block-K passages and write a one-line summary of each in Korean, 15 words maximum.
3. Build a personal list of 200 words that have appeared in block-K passages and that you did not know. Sort by frequency across papers.

### Module completion gate

- You solve insert-the-sentence questions at ≥ 80% accuracy.
- You can identify the first sentence of an ordering question in under 15 seconds.
- You have a 200-word block-K vocabulary list in your SRS.

## 15.6 — 쓰기 Q51–Q52: the twenty points you cannot afford to lose

**Objective.** Secure the two sentence-completion questions. They are worth 20 points, they take four minutes, and they have a fixed grammar.

### Module 15.6.1 — What the questions are

| Question | Format | Points | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Q51** | A short practical text (a notice, an advertisement, an email) with two blanks | 10 (5 + 5) | one clause per blank |
| **Q52** | The same format, slightly longer, more formal | 10 (5 + 5) | one clause per blank |

### Module 15.6.2 — The rules that are actually scored

| Rule | Consequence of breaking it |
|---|---|
| **Match the register of the passage** | a `-습니다` clause in a 해요체 notice loses the point even if perfect |
| **Use the grammar the blank demands** | if the preceding clause is `-는데`, your clause must respect that relation |
| **Do not write a sentence** | you are completing a clause; a full stop in the middle loses the point |
| **Do not repeat the passage's words** | the blank is not a summarising exercise |
| **No spelling errors** | one misspelling typically costs the point, not half of it |

### Module 15.6.3 — The four blank-types

| Type | The cue | The answer shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | `-기 위해`, `-려고` | `-기 위해서입니다` / `-려고 합니다` |
| **Condition** | `-면`, `-다면` | `-을 경우에는` / `-다면` |
| **Reason** | `-기 때문에`, `-어서` | `-기 때문입니다` |
| **Contrast or caveat** | `-지만`, `-는데` | `-지만` + the reversed fact |

### Module 15.6.4 — The four-minute drill

**Minute 1:** read both passages and identify the register. Write it in the margin.
**Minute 2:** identify the blank type for each of the four blanks.
**Minutes 3–4:** write, then proofread for register, spelling, and the final verb ending.

### Practice tasks

1. Take ten Q51–52 pairs from past papers. For each, write the register and blank type before writing the answer.
2. Have a tutor mark them against the official rubric, not against "does it sound right".
3. Write twenty of them in one sitting, timed at two minutes per question.

### Module completion gate

- 18 of 20 on a set of ten Q51–52 pairs, marked by someone else.
- You never lose a point to register mismatch across a full mock.
- You finish both in four minutes with ninety seconds left for proofreading.

## 15.7 — 쓰기 Q53: describing data in 300 characters

**Objective.** Learn the single most formulaic piece of writing in the exam, and get 25 of its 30 points.

### Module 15.7.1 — What Q53 is

You are shown a graph, chart, or table — often with a survey premise — and asked to describe it in **200–300 characters**. It is worth **30 points**.

> **The rule that decides everything:** you describe the data. You do not explain it, interpret it, or give an opinion about it. A single sentence beginning `이는 ... 때문인 것으로 보인다` is a deduction the rubric does not reward and can cost you points for straying from the task.

### Module 15.7.2 — The structure

| Block | Characters | Function | Opening formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Frame** | 40–60 | what the data is, when, of whom | `조사 결과에 따르면, ...` |
| **2. Main figure** | 80–100 | the largest or the headline number | `가장 높게 나타났으며` / `가장 많았다` |
| **3. Contrast** | 60–80 | the second figure, ideally compared to the first | `반면에`, `이에 비해` |
| **4. Trend or outlier** | 40–60 | the change, the smallest, the exception | `한편`, `반면` |

### Module 15.7.3 — The phrases that earn points

| Function | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Introducing the data | `조사 결과에 따르면` `자료에 의하면` `다음은 ...을 조사한 것이다` |
| Reporting a high | `가장 높은 것으로 나타났다` `가장 많은 비율을 차지했다` |
| Reporting a low | `가장 낮게 조사되었다` `미미한 수준에 그쳤다` |
| Comparing | `-에 비해 두 배 이상 높았다` `-보다 약간 높은 수준이었다` |
| Change over time | `-년에 비해 증가했다` `꾸준히 감소하는 추세를 보였다` |
| Caveating | `다만, -은 예외적으로 낮았다` |

### Module 15.7.4 — The four errors

| Error | Why it costs points |
|---|---|
| **Giving a reason** | the task says describe; the rubric rewards relevance to the task |
| **Leaving out a category** | the rubric requires all major items, even briefly |
| **Under the character count** | below 200 characters reads as an incomplete response |
| **Repeating the same verb** | `나타났다` five times reads as a low proficiency; vary it |

### Practice tasks

1. Take six past Q53 prompts. Write each in exactly 250 characters. Count characters, not words.
2. Write the same prompt three ways: emphasising the largest figure, the change over time, and the contrast. Notice the task usually specifies which.
3. Have each marked against the official 30-point rubric, and record which of the four blocks lost points.

### Module completion gate

- Six Q53 responses, all between 220 and 290 characters, none containing a causal explanation.
- ≥ 24/30 on a marked response.
- You can write a Q53 in eleven minutes, leaving four for checking.

## 15.8 — 쓰기 Q54: the 700-character essay

**Objective.** Build one essay structure that you can deploy against any prompt, in 30 minutes, and score 35 of 50.

### Module 15.8.1 — What Q54 is

A short topic statement — usually a social issue with two sides or a proposed change — and an instruction to give your opinion with reasons. **600–700 characters. 50 points. About 30 minutes.**

### Module 15.8.2 — The rubric, in the order it is scored

Scoring is on three axes. Candidates obsess over grammar and ignore the first two, which are worth more.

| Axis | What it means | Typical weight |
|---|---|---|
| **내용** Content | Did you answer *the question asked*, with reasons and examples relevant to it? | a third |
| **구성** Structure | Is there an introduction, a body with distinct points, and a conclusion that closes? | a third |
| **언어 사용** Language | Grammar accuracy, vocabulary range, spelling, appropriate register | a third |

> **The insight:** an essay with simple but correct grammar and a clear two-reason structure beats an essay with ambitious grammar and one reason. Difficulty is not rewarded. Completion is.

### Module 15.8.3 — The four-block structure

| Block | Characters | What it does | How it opens |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. 서론** | 100–120 | restate the issue, state your position | `최근 ...이 늘면서 ...에 대한 논의가 활발해지고 있다. 나는 ...라고 생각한다.` |
| **2. 근거 1** | 200–240 | strongest reason plus a concrete example | `첫째, ... 때문이다. 예를 들어, ...` |
| **3. 근거 2 or 반론** | 200–240 | second reason, or the opposing view and why it fails | `둘째, ... / 한편, ...라는 주장도 있다. 그러나 ...` |
| **4. 결론** | 80–100 | restate the position, add a forward-looking close | `따라서 ...해야 할 것이다.` |

### Module 15.8.4 — The twenty phrases worth memorising

| Function | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Opening | `최근 들어`, `급격히 증가하고 있다`, `논란이 되고 있다` |
| Stating a position | `-라고 생각한다`, `-은 분명하다`, `-해야 한다고 본다` |
| Enumerating | `첫째`, `둘째`, `무엇보다도` |
| Exemplifying | `예를 들어`, `-ㄴ 사례가 대표적이다` |
| Conceding | `물론 -ㄹ 수도 있다`, `-라는 점은 인정한다` |
| Rebutting | `그러나`, `하지만 이는 -다는 점을 간과한 것이다` |
| Concluding | `따라서`, `이를 통해`, `-해야 할 것이다` |

### Module 15.8.5 — The essay rules that are not about grammar

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| Use 합니다체 or 해라체, never mix | mixed register is the most common deduction |
| Never use 반말, even accidentally | it reads as a register error, not a typo |
| One idea per paragraph | the 구성 score rewards visible structure |
| Write 650 characters, not 600 | the lower bound is a floor, not a target |
| Do not write a title | it wastes characters and gains nothing |
| Leave two minutes to check spelling | `-되` vs `-돼` and `-로서` vs `-로써` are exam favourites |

### Practice tasks

1. Write the same prompt twice: once with two reasons, once with one reason and a rebuttal. Have both marked. Learn which structure your marker rewards.
2. Write ten prompts in thirty minutes each, across two weeks. Increase the count to two per session by week four.
3. Build a personal bank of ten examples — Korean-specific, concrete, and reusable across prompts (an education policy, a workplace case, a technology change, a demographic fact).

### Module completion gate

- Ten timed essays, all 600–700 characters, all scoring ≥ 35/50.
- Zero register mixing across three consecutive essays.
- You can plan a Q54 in four minutes on a prompt you have never seen.

## 15.9 — The vocabulary and grammar the exam actually reuses

**Objective.** Stop studying Korean broadly. Study the exam's own corpus.

### Module 15.9.1 — The academic verb set

TOPIK reading and listening are built from a small pool of formal verbs. Learn them as collocations, not as words.

| Verb | Collocation | Meaning in exam context |
|---|---|---|
| `나타나다` | `-ㄴ 것으로 나타났다` | the standard reporting verb of survey data |
| `드러나다` | `-ㄴ 것으로 드러났다` | something was revealed — usually negative |
| `밝히다` | `-다고 밝혔다` | an institution stated |
| `지적하다` | `-다고 지적했다` | a critic pointed out — implies fault |
| `강조하다` | `-다고 강조했다` | urged, emphasised |
| `주장하다` | `-다고 주장했다` | claimed — often contested |
| `기대되다` | `-ㄹ 것으로 기대된다` | is expected to |
| `우려되다` | `-ㄹ 것으로 우려된다` | is feared to |
| `판단되다` | `-ㄴ다고 판단된다` | is judged to |
| `요구되다` | `-ㄴ다고 요구된다` | is required |

> **Why this matters:** in block K, roughly one question per paper turns on distinguishing `주장했다` from `밝혔다`. One is a claim, the other is a statement of fact by an institution, and the correct answer depends on which.

### Module 15.9.2 — The connector ladder

| Relation | Formal | Semi-formal | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cause | `-므로`, `-기 때문에` | `-어서` | `-므로` cannot be followed by an imperative |
| Contrast | `-나`, `-지만` | `-는데` | `-는데` can also mean background, not contrast |
| Condition | `-다면`, `-을 경우` | `-면` | `-다면` implies counterfactual more often |
| Concession | `-더라도`, `-ㄹ지라도` | `-아도` | `-더라도` is stronger and more formal |
| Addition | `-뿐만 아니라`, `-에 더해` | `-고` | `-고` chains do not imply sequence |

### Module 15.9.3 — The register audit

TOPIK passages mix registers deliberately. Before answering any question about a long passage, identify its register in the margin:

| Ending | Register | Typical source |
|---|---|---|
| `-다`, `-ㄴ다` | 해라체 | newspapers, academic writing, editorials |
| `-습니다`, `-ㅂ니다` | 합니다체 | announcements, formal speech, official documents |
| `-어요`, `-아요` | 해요체 | conversation, advertisements, friendly notices |
| `-더라`, `-더군요` | hearsay/recollection | narrative, personal account |

**One mark in the margin, every passage, every time.** Register errors are where most candidates lose block-G points, and register identification is a two-second habit.

### Practice tasks

1. Extract every `-다고 ...했다` construction from five past papers. Build a verb list with the noun it governs.
2. Take ten block-K passages and label the register of each in one word before answering.
3. Build a 300-item exam vocabulary deck from the words that repeat across three papers — not from a general word list.

### Module completion gate

- You can produce the ten academic reporting verbs with their collocations, cold.
- You identify register in under three seconds on any passage.
- Your exam deck contains 300 words sourced from real papers, not from a textbook.

## 15.11 — If you already have TOPIK 5: the last thirty points

**Objective.** For candidates stuck in the 190–229 band, identify where the missing points actually live.

### Module 15.11.1 — The gap analysis

Almost nobody is stuck because of listening. The distribution of the missing 30 points is remarkably consistent:

| Source of lost points | Typical shortfall | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **쓰기 Q54** | 15–20 | under-length, one reason only, or register mixing |
| **읽기 block K** | 10–14 | running out of time, guessing the last ten |
| **듣기 block H** | 4–8 | losing place in monologues |
| **Vocabulary in block K** | 3–6 | unknown academic nouns |

**Conclusion: the fix is almost always 쓰기 and reading speed, in that order.**

### Module 15.11.2 — The three-week intervention

| Week | Focus | Daily drill |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Q54 structure | one 30-minute essay per day, marked |
| 2 | 읽기 speed | blocks A–G in 25 minutes, daily, plus five block-K passages |

### Module 15.11.3 — What does not work

| Approach | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Memorising essay templates verbatim | markers recognise them; content score collapses when the template does not fit the prompt |

# LEVEL 16 — TRANSLATION AND INTERPRETATION

> **Goal.** Move meaning between Korean and one other language without losing the things that make it mean. By the end you can translate a 3,000-word document with a translator's note explaining every hard choice, and perform fifteen minutes of consecutive interpreting from audio you have never heard.

## Level dashboard

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Level** | 16 — Translation and Interpretation |
| **Difficulty** | Near-native |
| **Suggested duration** | 8–12 weeks |
| **Study commitment** | 2–3 hours per day |
| **Core tools** | Parallel corpora, a glossary spreadsheet, a recorder, an interpreting tutor, published translations to compare against |
| **Main project** | **`bridge-kit`** — a translation portfolio, a 300-term glossary, and a recorded consecutive performance |
| **Final outcome** | Three translated texts with notes, a 15-minute consecutive interpretation at ≥ 80% content accuracy, and a 300-term glossary. |

**Prerequisites.** Level 15 complete. In practice: TOPIK 6 level (230+), and — the part that actually matters — you can read a Korean sentence and say what it *does*, not only what it says.

> **The honesty clause.** Your competence is asymmetric, and it will stay asymmetric for years. Korean → your native language will be far stronger than your native language → Korean, because the second direction requires you to write Korean that a Korean would have written, and that is the hardest thing in this roadmap. Plan your portfolio around the strong direction and train the weak one deliberately.

## 16.1 — What translation is

**Objective.** Replace the amateur model ("carry the words across") with the professional one ("rebuild the sentence in the target language so it does the same thing").

### Module 16.1.1 — 번역 and 통역

| | 번역 Translation | 통역 Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Medium** | written, revisable | spoken, unrevisable |
| **Time** | minutes to days per sentence | seconds |
| **The enemy** | imprecision | latency |
| **The skill** | rewriting | reformulating under pressure |
| **How you fail** | a correct sentence that reads like a translation | a pause long enough to lose the room |

### Module 16.1.2 — The four things that must survive

A translation succeeds if these four cross the bridge. They are listed in order of how often they are abandoned.

| # | What | What it looks like when lost |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Propositional meaning** | the facts are wrong — a hard failure, rare |
| **2** | **Register** | a corporate apology reads like a text message, or a poem reads like a manual |
| **3** | **Implicature** | the politeness, the threat, the joke, the reluctance — all gone |
| **4** | **Rhythm** | the sentence is correct and unreadable; a native would never have written it |

> **The amateur trade:** sacrifice 2, 3, and 4 to preserve 1. **The professional trade:** sacrifice surface form to preserve all four.

### Module 16.1.3 — The unit is the sentence, and sometimes the paragraph

Literal translation fails because Korean and English do not share sentence boundaries. A Korean sentence may contain four clauses that English would split into three sentences. A single English sentence may collapse into one Korean clause plus a trailing `-는데`.

**The rule:** if you find yourself translating clause by clause and the result is grammatical but strange, the boundary is wrong, not the words.

### Practice tasks

1. Take one paragraph of Korean newspaper prose and one of English newspaper prose on the same event. Compare sentence counts. Notice the ratio.
2. Translate a 150-word Korean paragraph three ways: maximally literal, maximally free, and balanced. Have a native read all three and tell you which one they would have written.
3. Write down, in one sentence each, what you think the four invariants mean for a business email.

### Module completion gate

- You can state the four invariants without notes.
- You can identify which invariant a given bad translation sacrificed.
- You have produced three versions of one paragraph and can justify the balanced one.

## 16.2 — Register across the bridge

**Objective.** Solve the problem that sinks more translations than vocabulary: Korean has a grammar of social relationship and English mostly does not.

### Module 16.2.1 — The honorific economy

| Korean resource | English has | The consequence |
|---|---|---|
| `-시-` subject honorific | nothing systematic | must be carried by verb choice or context |
| `-습니다` vs `-어요` vs `-다` | "please", "would you", tone | register is often invisible in English until it is wrong |
| `선배`, `후배`, `과장님`, `사모님` | first names | a relationship becomes a name |
| `우리` | "my" or "our" | `우리 어머니` is "my mother", not "our mother" |
| `드리다`, `모시다`, `뵙다` | "give", "take", "meet" | the humility is lost unless the verb is upgraded |

### Module 16.2.2 — The default decisions

| Korean source | Into English | Never |
|---|---|---|
| `-습니다` in a business letter | formal, complete sentences, no contractions | "Hi" or "Hey" |
| `-어요` in a customer notice | neutral, friendly, contractions acceptable | bureaucratic passives |
| `-다` in an editorial | declarative, no hedges, present tense | "I think that" |
| `-더라` narration | past tense plus a hint of personal witness ("I remember", "I found") | bare past tense that loses the witnessing |
| `반말` between close friends | contractions, short clauses, no honorifics | anything stilted |

### Module 16.2.3 — The English "you" problem

English requires a subject. Korean drops it. When you translate into Korean, you must decide — every time — whether `you` becomes `너`, `당신`, `여러분`, a title, or nothing at all.

| English | Wrong Korean | Right Korean | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| "You should apply early." | `당신은 일찍 신청해야 합니다.` | `일찍 신청하시는 것이 좋습니다.` | `당신` is confrontational outside arguments and translations |
| "We need your feedback." | `우리는 당신의 의견이 필요합니다.` | `고객님의 의견을 기다리고 있습니다.` | the title carries the relationship |
| "You never listen." | `당신은 절대 듣지 않아요.` | `내 말을 좀 들어 봐요.` | drop the pronoun; let the verb carry it |

> **The rule:** in Korean, `당신` and `너` are *marked*. Their absence is the default. If your Korean translation is full of `당신`, it was translated from English.

### Practice tasks

1. Take ten English sentences containing "you" and translate them into Korean without using `당신` once. Justify each choice.
2. Take a Korean customer-service notice in 해요체 and a corporate apology in 합니다체. Translate both into English and make the register difference visible to a reader who does not know Korean.
3. Find five Korean sentences with `-시-` and translate them so that a reader can tell who is being honoured.

### Module completion gate

- Zero instances of `당신` in a 500-character Korean translation, unless deliberately marked.
- A native reader can tell your 해요체 from your 합니다체 translation without seeing the source.
- You can name the register of any Korean passage on sight.

## 16.3 — Korean into English: three structural problems

**Objective.** Learn the three transformations that make Korean→English translation readable, because this is the direction you will actually be paid for first.

### Module 16.3.1 — Topic-prominence

Korean marks topic (`-은/는`) and subject (`-이/가`) separately. English marks only subject. The consequence: Korean sentences begin with a topic that is not the grammatical subject, and a literal translation produces a sentence with no engine.

| Korean | Literal (wrong) | Rebuilt |
|---|---|---|
| `이 문제는 정부가 해결해야 한다.` | "This problem, the government must solve." | "The government must solve this problem." |
| `그 영화는 내가 봤다.` | "That movie, I saw." | "I've seen that movie." |
| `한국은 겨울이 춥다.` | "Korea, winter is cold." | "Winters in Korea are cold." |

**The method:** find the `-이/가` phrase. That is your English subject. The `-은/는` phrase becomes the object, the time expression, or the place.

### Module 16.3.2 — Pro-drop and the missing subject

Korean drops subjects, objects, and sometimes verbs entirely. English cannot. When you translate, you must *supply* what Korean omitted — and get it wrong about 20% of the time if you are careless.

**The three questions to ask of every clause:**
1. Who is doing this?
2. What are they doing it to?
3. Is this a new fact or a continuation?

If a Korean passage says `그렇게 하기로 했다` with no subject, the answer is in the previous sentence, and you must carry it forward into English.

### Module 16.3.3 — The pre-nominal modifier chain

This is the hardest structural feature of Korean, and the one that makes machine translation fail visibly.

```
그가 작년에 처음 만났다고 말했던 그 사람이 지금 우리 회사에서 일하고 있다.
```

Everything before `그 사람` is a relative clause stacked onto a noun. English cannot do this. The **unpack-and-rebuild** method:

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Find the head noun — here `그 사람` |
| 2 | List the clauses attached to it, in order |
| 3 | Decide which becomes a relative clause, which becomes a separate sentence, which becomes an appositive |
| 4 | Rebuild in English order — usually head noun first, then clauses to the right |

**Result:** "The man he said he'd first met last year now works at our company." Or, better, split: "He said he'd first met the man last year. That man now works at our company."

> **Rule of thumb:** a Korean modifier chain longer than three clauses becomes two English sentences. Native English writers do not stack; they serialise.

### Practice tasks

1. Take ten Korean sentences with `-은/는` topics that are not subjects. Translate them with the `-이/가` rule and check against a published translation.
2. Take five modifier chains of three or more clauses and unpack each into two or three English sentences.
3. Take a published Korean→English translation of a novel paragraph and back-translate it into Korean. Compare with the original. Note every place the translator chose differently from the source structure.

### Module completion gate

- Ten topic-prominence sentences translated without a single "As for..." construction.
- Five modifier chains unpacked, none exceeding two clauses in English.
- A back-translation that recovers ≥ 90% of the original's propositions.

## 16.4 — English into Korean: the harder direction

**Objective.** Build the "Koreanify" pass — the revision stage that turns a grammatical Korean translation into one a Korean would have written.

### Module 16.4.1 — Definiteness

English has `a`, `the`, and zero article. Korean has `이`, `그`, `저`, and — most often — nothing.

| English | Wrong Korean | Right Korean | The rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| "I bought a book." | `나는 그 책을 샀다.` | `책을 한 권 샀다.` | first mention takes no determiner; use a counter |
| "The book was expensive." | `책은 비쌌다.` | `그 책은 비쌌다.` | second mention takes `그` |
| "Books are expensive." | `그 책들은 비싸다.` | `책은 비싸다.` | generic takes `-은/는` with no plural |

**The rule:** English articles map to Korean *information structure*, not to Korean words. First mention → no marker or a counter. Second mention → `그`. Generic → bare noun with `-은/는`.

### Module 16.4.2 — Relative clauses

English puts relatives to the right of the noun; Korean puts them to the left and without a relative pronoun. This is where machine translation produces Korean that is technically parseable and completely foreign.

| English | Machine-flavoured | Koreanified |
|---|---|---|
| "The report that the team submitted last week was rejected." | `지난주에 팀이 제출한 그 보고서는 거절되었다.` | `팀이 지난주에 제출한 보고서가 반려됐다.` |
| "Anyone who wants to join should sign up." | `참여하고 싶은 사람은 누구나 신청해야 한다.` | `참여를 원하는 분은 신청해 주시기 바랍니다.` |
| "A policy that would help small businesses" | `작은 사업을 도울 정책` | `소상공인을 지원하는 정책` |

**Three fixes:** delete the `그`; choose the correct particle (`-이/가` vs `-은/는`) by information structure; and replace the literal verb with the collocation Koreans actually use (`거절되다` → `반려되다` in a corporate document).

### Module 16.4.3 — Voice

English prefers passive in formal writing; Korean prefers active with a plural subject, or the `-어지다` / `-게 되다` construction, or an impersonal noun.

| English | Stiff Korean | Natural Korean |
|---|---|---|
| "It was decided that the meeting would be postponed." | `회의가 연기되는 것이 결정되었다.` | `회의를 연기하기로 했다.` |
| "Mistakes were made." | `실수들이 행해졌다.` | `실수가 있었습니다.` |
| "The system is being updated." | `시스템이 업데이트되고 있는 중이다.` | `시스템 점검 중입니다.` |

### Module 16.4.4 — The Koreanify pass

After a first draft, run four checks in this order. They catch about 80% of translationese.

| Pass | Question | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Pronoun sweep** | are there `그`, `그녀`, `그들`, `당신`? | delete most; Korean repeats the noun or drops it |
| **2. Particle check** | does every `-은/는` mark a topic, and every `-이/가` a new fact? | swap the ones that are decorative |
| **3. Verb upgrade** | is the verb a translation of an English verb, or the verb a Korean would use? | `만들다` → `조성하다`, `보다` → `살펴보다` |
| **4. Ending check** | does every sentence end the way this register ends? | `-입니다` in a report, `-한다` in an editorial |

### Practice tasks

1. Translate 500 words of an English news article into Korean, then run the four-pass Koreanify. Keep both drafts and count the changes.
2. Collect ten sentences of published English→Korean translation (from a bilingual book or a company website) and mark the places where the translator Koreanified rather than translated literally.
3. Have a native read your draft aloud and stop you at every sentence they would not have written. Those are your real errors, and they are usually not grammar.

### Module completion gate

- A 500-word translation with fewer than five sentences a native would not have written.
- Zero unnecessary `그` / `그녀`.
- You can run the four passes in under twenty minutes on 500 words.

## 16.5 — Terminology: the glossary is the deliverable

**Objective.** Treat terminology as engineering, not vocabulary. Consistency is worth more than elegance.

### Module 16.5.1 — Why consistency beats quality

In a 40,000-word technical document, a term translated two ways is a defect, even if both are defensible. The reader builds a mental map; an inconsistent map costs them comprehension.

**The 10-term rule:** if a document uses a technical term more than ten times, it goes in the glossary before you translate sentence one.

### Module 16.5.2 — The three-source rule

Never accept a dictionary's first suggestion. Verify every term against three independent sources.

| Source | What it gives you | Its failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| **Dictionary** (표준국어대사전, 국립국어원) | the official definition and the standard rendering | it is often the schoolbook version, not the industry version |
| **Industry usage** (the client's own documents, competitor sites, trade press) | what practitioners actually say | it may be inconsistent, and may be wrong |
| **Parallel text** (a bilingual document using the same term) | proof that the term functions in context | parallel texts are scarce outside a few domains |

**When the three disagree:** the client's usage wins for the client's document, and you flag it in the note.

### Module 16.5.3 — The glossary template

| Column | Example |
|---|---|
| Source term | `stakeholder` |
| Target term | `이해관계자` |
| Part of speech | noun |
| Definition | a party affected by a decision |
| Approved variant | `이해당사자` (not used in this client's materials) |
| Forbidden | `주주` — too narrow |
| Source of decision | client style guide, 2024 |
| Date | 2026-03-14 |

### Module 16.5.4 — The five terminology traps

| Trap | Example | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **False friend** | `선물` is a gift, not "a present" in the financial sense | check the domain before the dictionary |
| **Near-synonym drift** | `개선` vs `향상` vs `보완` | gloss them separately, with the verb they take |
| **Acronym trap** | `AI` is fine; `KPI` needs `핵심성과지표` on first use | expand on first occurrence |
| **Legacy term** | `전자상거래` vs `이커머스` in the same client's materials | follow the client, note the inconsistency |
| **Loan-word preference** | `컨설팅` over `자문` in business, but `자문` in law | domain decides, not the dictionary |

### Practice tasks

1. Build a 300-term glossary in one domain — pick one and stay in it (legal, medical, semiconductor, or game localisation).
2. For each term, record all three sources and the decision.
3. Translate a 1,000-word document using only glossary-approved terms, and count your deviations.

### Module completion gate

- A 300-term glossary with three sources per entry.
- A 1,000-word translation with zero unflagged terminological deviations.
- You can explain why consistency outranks elegance in a technical document.

## 16.6 — The untranslatable: 정, 한, 눈치, and the rest

**Objective.** Learn the four strategies for culture-bound terms, and when each one is right.

### Module 16.6.1 — The core set

| Term | The nearest gloss | Why a gloss fails |
|---|---|---|
| **정** | attachment, affection built through proximity | it is not love, and it is not friendship; it accumulates |
| **한** | deep unresolved grief or resentment | it is collective and historical as much as personal |
| **눈치** | the ability to read a situation without being told | "social awareness" loses the speed and the self-interest |
| **우리** | we, our | `우리 엄마` is "my mum"; it is not a possessive but a belonging |
| **밥** | rice, a meal | `밥 먹었어?` is a greeting, not a question about food |
| **눈치채다** | to notice | implies noticing what was not meant to be noticed |
| **서운하다** | disappointed, mildly hurt | always relational, never about events |

### Module 16.6.2 — The four strategies

| Strategy | What you do | Use when | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transliterate** | keep `정` in italics, gloss once | the text is about the concept itself | reads as exotic in a news piece |
| **Calque** | `han`, "the grief of the unresolved" | the reader needs the texture | can sound precious |
| **Functional equivalent** | `눈치` → "he read the room" | the function matters more than the name | loses the cultural specificity |
| **Omit and compensate** | drop it, add the nuance elsewhere | it appears once and carries no argument | impossible if the term recurs |

### Module 16.6.3 — The decision procedure

1. **Does the term carry an argument?** If yes, it must be visible. Transliterate or calque.
2. **Does it appear once?** If yes, functional equivalent is safest.
3. **Is the reader an outsider?** Then gloss at first use, once, in parentheses.
4. **Is the reader an insider?** Then never gloss; they will find it condescending.

### Practice tasks

1. Translate a 300-word Korean essay about 정 into English twice: once transliterating, once with a functional equivalent. Have a bilingual reader judge which serves the essay.
2. Collect ten Korean culture-bound terms and assign a strategy to each, with a written reason.
3. Find a published translation of a Korean novel and catalogue how the translator handled the food vocabulary.

### Module completion gate

- Ten terms, each with a strategy and a justification.
- Two complete versions of one culture-bound passage, and a defensible choice between them.
- You can explain the outsider/insider gloss rule without notes.

## 16.7 — Consecutive interpreting

**Objective.** Carry meaning across a gap of thirty seconds to three minutes, from speech you have never heard, without notes that look like shorthand for nothing.

### Module 16.7.1 — The formats

| Format | The gap | Typical setting | The skill |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liaison** | one or two sentences | business meeting, clinic, immigration | short-term memory, politeness transfer |
| **Consecutive** | 1–5 minutes | conference Q&A, negotiation, site visit | note-taking, structure recovery |
| **Simultaneous** | ~2 seconds | conference booth | split attention, near-automaticity |
| **Whispered (chuchotage)** | ~2 seconds | one listener, no booth | quietness and speed |

**This level targets consecutive.** Simultaneous requires a dedicated programme and is out of scope here — but note that consecutive practice is what makes simultaneous possible later.

### Module 16.7.2 — The note-taking system

You are not writing words. You are writing **structure**: who, did what, to whom, why, and the connector between ideas.

| Element | How to write it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Noun** | first letters or a symbol | `mf` = manufacturing; `₩` = budget |
| **Verb** | an arrow or a mark | `↑` rise, `→` leads to, `⊥` block |
| **Subject shift** | a new line | a new speaker or agent gets a new line, always |
| **Connector** | in the left margin | `but`, `so`, `e.g.`, `?` for hedging |
| **Numbers** | digits plus unit | `3.2만` — write digits, never spell |
| **Negation** | a diagonal slash through the line | the single most dangerous thing to miss |
| **Names and titles** | full first syllable | names cannot be reconstructed from initials |

**The layout rule:** notes go down the page in a vertical line, one idea per line, connectors in the left margin. This is what lets you read your notes back in the order the speaker said things.

### Module 16.7.3 — The three failures

| Failure | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Note-blindness** | you have notes and cannot read them | simplify the notation; practise reading notes 10 minutes later |
| **Lag collapse** | you fall 90 seconds behind and stop hearing | drop to the gist; a summarised segment beats a lost one |
| **False fluency** | you say something confident and wrong | when uncertain, say "the speaker said X, and I want to confirm the figure" — never invent |

> **The interpreter's first duty is not to lie.** A confident wrong number in a negotiation is worse than a request to repeat. Practise the sentence that buys you time, in both languages, until it is automatic.

### Module 16.7.4 — The 30-second drill ladder

| Rung | Source | Gap | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | a news bulletin, your native language | 30 s | 95% content |
| 2 | a news bulletin, Korean | 30 s | 85% |
| 3 | a 2-minute monologue, native language | 2 min | 90% |
| 4 | a 2-minute Korean monologue | 2 min | 80% |
| 5 | a 5-minute Korean talk with numbers and names | 5 min | 75%, no invented facts |

**Record every attempt.** The gap between what you think you said and what you actually said is where the learning is.

### Practice tasks

1. Build your notation system on paper and commit to it for two weeks. Do not change it mid-drill.
2. Do rung 1 and rung 2 daily for a week. Score yourself against the source transcript.
3. Record a 3-minute consecutive performance of a Korean news segment and have a bilingual listener mark it for accuracy, completeness, and delivery.

### Module completion gate

- A notation system you can read back after 10 minutes with ≥ 90% accuracy.
- Rung 4 at ≥ 80% content accuracy, three times.
- Zero invented facts across five recorded performances.

## 16.8 — Sight translation and the timed pressure of the booth

**Objective.** Translate a written text aloud, cold, in real time — the format most often used in Korean public-sector and corporate screening.

### Module 16.8.1 — What sight translation adds

You cannot revise, you cannot search, and the audience hears your thinking. The skill is **chunking**: read a clause, hold it, say it, move on — while reading the next clause with the other eye.

| Skill | Why it is separate from translation |
|---|---|
| Chunking | you must break the sentence *before* you know how it ends |
| Risk management | a hard term must be handled without a pause |
| Delivery | hesitation reads as incompetence, even when the content is right |

### Module 16.8.2 — The preparation minute

You are usually given 1–3 minutes with the text before speaking. Spend it like this:

| Minute | Action |
|---|---|
| first 20 s | read the title, the first sentence, and the last sentence — that is the argument |
| next 20 s | scan for numbers, names, and terms — mark them, because they cannot be improvised |
| remaining | read the first two paragraphs closely; the rest you will read cold |

### Module 16.8.3 — The hard-term protocol

When you hit a term you do not know, you have three options, in this order of preference:

1. **Circumlocute**: "a type of chemical used in semiconductor cleaning" — say what it does.
2. **Preserve and move**: say the term in the source language and continue — legitimate in a technical setting.
3. **Flag**: "the exact term here is technical; I'll come back to it" — and do come back.

**Never:** invent a term that sounds plausible. A wrong drug name or a wrong legal term is a real-world harm.

### Practice tasks

1. Sight-translate one Korean newspaper article per day for two weeks, recorded, cold.
2. Sight-translate a legal or medical text and use the circumlocution protocol on every unknown term.
3. Have a bilingual listener mark your delivery for hesitation markers and fillers.

### Module completion gate

- Ten recorded sight translations.
- Fewer than three invented terms across all ten.
- Delivery with no pause longer than four seconds.

## 16.9 — Editing, revision, and the translator's note

**Objective.** Learn the second half of the job: a first draft is 60% of a translation, and the note is what a client actually pays for on a hard text.

### Module 16.9.1 — The three-pass revision

| Pass | What you look for | What you must not do |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Accuracy** | omissions, additions, wrong numbers, wrong names, mistranslated negation | do not fix style yet |
| **2. Fluency** | sentences the target reader would not write | do not re-check accuracy |
| **3. Register and consistency** | glossary adherence, honorific level, tense | do not rewrite content |

**Read the target text alone on pass 2.** If you read source and target side by side you will see what you meant to write, not what is there.

### Module 16.9.2 — The translator's note

A proper note has four parts, in this order:

| Part | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Brief** | audience, purpose, register of the source and target | "a corporate apology, addressed to customers, formal in both languages" |
| **2. Decisions** | the three to five hard choices and why | "정 rendered as 'attachment' rather than 'affection' to avoid romantic connotation" |
| **3. Losses** | what could not be carried | "the honorific distinction between the CEO and the manager is not recoverable in English" |
| **4. Open questions** | what you would ask the client | "whether the date in paragraph 3 is the signing or the effective date" |

> **Why the note matters more than it looks:** a client who can see your reasoning can defend the translation to their own boss. A translator who never states a decision gets overruled on style by someone who has not read the source.

### Module 16.9.3 — The things a client actually complains about

| Complaint | Real cause |
|---|---|
| "It doesn't sound Korean" | translationese — the four-pass Koreanify was skipped |
| "The tone is wrong" | register was not established before translating |
| "The term is wrong" | no glossary, or the glossary was built from a dictionary |
| "It's too long / too short" | sentence boundaries were preserved instead of restructured |
| "It's fine but we can't use it" | the purpose of the text was never asked about |

### Practice tasks

1. Translate a 1,000-word text and submit it with a full four-part note.
2. Have a native read the target text and mark every sentence they would rewrite. Count how many are accuracy errors versus fluency errors. The ratio tells you what to work on.
3. Take a published translation and write the translator's note it would have had.

### Module completion gate

- Three translations with complete notes.
- A revision cycle where pass 2 finds no accuracy errors that pass 1 missed.
- One translation a professional would sign.

## 16.10 — The tools, and their limits

**Objective.** Use CAT tools and machine translation without letting them flatten your judgement.

### Module 16.10.1 — What CAT tools actually do

| Feature | What it does | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|
| **Translation memory** | reuses your past segments | understand meaning |
| **Term base** | enforces the glossary | choose between variants |
| **Alignment** | links source and target sentences | fix a bad alignment |
| **QA check** | flags numbers, tags, missing segments | judge register |
| **Segment filter** | shows untranslated or long segments | know which segment is hard |

### Module 16.10.2 — The machine-translation protocol

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Read the source and decide whether it is machine-appropriate (a manual, yes; a poem, no) |
| 2 | Machine-translate, then **never accept a segment without reading the source beside it** |
| 3 | Use the output as a first draft, not a translation |
| 4 | Run the four-pass Koreanify on every machine segment |
| 5 | Log every recurring machine error in your glossary — the machines repeat themselves, and so do their mistakes |

**The error signature of Korean machine translation:**

| Error | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong pronoun | `당신` everywhere | English `you` mapped literally |
| Definite article overuse | `그` before every noun | English `the` mapped literally |
| Relative clause stacking | `-ㄴ` chains of four clauses | English structure preserved |
| Stiff voice | `-되는 것이 결정되었다` | English passive preserved |
| Honorific collapse | `-습니다` and `-어요` mixed | the model does not track speaker relationships |

### Module 16.10.3 — What you are still responsible for

Everything the tool cannot see: who the reader is, what the document is for, which of two equal readings is the intended one, and whether a sentence will embarrass the client. **The tool produces text. You produce a translation.**

### Practice tasks

1. Machine-translate 800 words, then edit it properly. Time both stages. Note which stage is longer — it is usually editing.
2. Catalogue fifteen machine errors by type using the table above.
3. Build a "machine error" sheet and add to it every week.

### Module completion gate

- An edited machine translation that a native cannot identify as machine-assisted.
- A 15-item error catalogue with types.
- A written rule for which document types you will not machine-translate.

## 16.11 — The portfolio, and the professional path

**Objective.** Turn the level's work into something you can send to a client, a publisher, or an employer.

### Module 16.11.1 — What goes in it

| Piece | Length | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| **A literary sample** | 1,500 words | demonstrates register control and voice |
| **A technical sample** | 1,500 words plus glossary | demonstrates consistency and terminology |
| **A commercial sample** | 500 words | demonstrates that you can write copy that sells |
| **A translator's note** | 600 words | demonstrates judgement — the thing that distinguishes you |
| **A consecutive recording** | 10 minutes | demonstrates that you can do it live |

### Module 16.11.2 — The rate arithmetic

Professional translation rates in Korea are usually quoted per **자** (character, in the Korean source) for Korean→foreign, and per word for foreign→Korean. At a beginner-professional stage, the more important number is your **daily capacity**: most translators produce 2,000–3,000 자 per day of publishable output, and a beginner produces half that.

**The rule for pricing your first jobs:** price the work, not the hours, and never accept a rate you cannot sustain over a month of full-time work.

### Module 16.11.3 — The three routes in

| Route | Path | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| **In-house** | a company's global team, a publisher, a broadcaster | a portfolio plus a test translation |
| **Freelance** | agency rosters, direct clients | a portfolio, a specialisation, and reliability |
| **Specialist** | legal, medical, patent, game localisation | domain knowledge plus a certification route |

### Practice tasks

1. Assemble the five portfolio pieces. Have a professional read them and tell you which one you should lead with.
2. Write a 200-word translator's bio in both languages.
3. Take one paid test translation, even at a low rate, and treat the deadline as absolute.

### Module completion gate

- A complete five-piece portfolio.
- One recorded consecutive performance in it.
- A written answer to "what is your specialisation, and why".

# PHASE 6 CAPSTONE — THE CREDENTIAL

> **The brief.** Produce both halves of the external proof in one fortnight: a real exam score, and a portfolio that would survive a professional's scrutiny. This capstone has no tutor-set topic. You choose the texts, and the only judge that counts is someone who does not know you.

Sit one full TOPIK II paper under exact conditions: same start time, same breaks, OMR sheet, no phone, no dictionary, no second attempt at a section.

|---|---|

Choose one text you would actually want to be known for — an essay, a short story extract, a technical document, or a set of poems — of **2,000 words in the target language**.

**Deliverable:** the translation plus a 700-word note.

| Standard | Threshold |
|---|---|

Fifteen minutes of consecutive interpreting, unscripted audio, one audience member who does not speak the source language.
