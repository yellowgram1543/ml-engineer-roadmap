# PHASE 7 — MASTERY

**Korean Roadmap · Levels 17–18 of 18**

> **Phase goal.** Close the distance that no exam can measure, and then build the machinery that keeps the language alive for the rest of your life.
>
> **The shift.** Every previous phase added capability. This one removes the last visible seams. There is no new grammar in L17 and almost no new vocabulary. What is left is timing, subtext, humour, cultural reference, and the hundred small choices that make a sentence sound like it came from a person rather than a learner.
>
> **The honest framing.** This is the phase that never ends, and it is the only one that cannot be completed by finishing a file. L17 has a finish line — a native speaker has to forget, for a measurable stretch of time, that you are foreign. L18 has none, because teaching and maintenance are practices, not milestones.

# LEVEL 17 — NATIVE-ADJACENT FLUENCY

> **Goal.** Be mistaken for a native for a sustained stretch — not on the phone for thirty seconds, and not because you said one idiom correctly, but for twenty minutes in a conversation you did not prepare.
>
> **What this level is not.** It is not accent elimination. Some learners reach near-native prosody and some never do, and the difference is mostly age of onset and ear. Accent is the least important thing in this level and the thing learners obsess over most. **Being mistaken for native and being native-adjacent are different targets, and only the second one is required here.**

## Level dashboard

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Level** | 17 — Native-Adjacent Fluency |
| **Difficulty** | Native-adjacent |
| **Suggested duration** | 4–8 months |
| **Study commitment** | 2 hours per day, at least half of it in live conversation |
| **Core tools** | Three native friends who will tell you the truth, a recorder you actually use, Korean-language media consumed for pleasure |
| **Main project** | **`fluency-proof`** — a 60-minute unprepared talk, a 3,000-word short story, and a native-blind test |
| **Final outcome** | Sixty minutes of speech with no preparation, a short story a Korean would finish reading, and a twenty-minute conversation in which the other person did not know. |

**Prerequisites.** Level 16 complete.

## 17.1 — What native-adjacent actually means

**Objective.** Replace a vague aspiration with a testable definition, because a goal you cannot measure will be chased forever.

### Module 17.1.1 — The five components

| Component | What it means | How it is tested |
|---|---|---|
| **Automaticity** | the sentence arrives without assembly | you speak for a minute without a visible search for grammar |
| **Timing** | you take your turn at the speed Koreans take theirs | no gap longer than 1.5 seconds in a three-way conversation |
| **Subtext** | you hear what was not said | you correctly identify a refusal that used no negative word |
| **Reference** | you catch the cultural allusion | you laugh at the right moment, not two seconds late |
| **Register agility** | you switch level mid-conversation without thinking | honour a superior and joke with a peer in the same minute |

### Module 17.1.2 — The five things that still mark you

These are what a Korean hears as foreign, in roughly descending order of how often they occur. None of them are grammatical.

| Marker | What it sounds like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Textbook completeness** | every sentence has a subject, verb, and object | drop pronouns; let the verb carry the meaning |
| **Evenness of stress** | every syllable gets equal weight | Korean is not stress-timed; the phrase carries the beat |
| **Formal connectors in speech** | `그러나`, `따라서` in conversation | `근데`, `그래서`, `그니까` |
| **Deliberate articulation** | too clear, too slow, no elision | learn the reductions in 17.2 |
| **Explaining too much** | saying the obvious, because you fear being misunderstood | trust the listener; cut a third |

### Module 17.1.3 — The native-blind test

The only honest measurement is this: **a Korean who does not know you talks to you for twenty minutes and does not ask where you are from.**

It is an unreliable test — a polite Korean may not ask even if they suspect. So use the four-probe version:

| Probe | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| **The phone call** | a stranger on the phone does not detect you (no visual cues) |
| **The group** | you follow a three-way conversation with overlapping speech and interruptions |
| **The joke** | you laugh before the punchline lands, because you saw it coming |
| **The correction** | a Korean corrects your Korean as if you were a native who misspoke, not a learner who erred |

> **The fourth probe is the real one.** When a Korean says `그거 아니고...` rather than `외국인이시죠?`, you have crossed. It means they have stopped modelling you as a learner.

### Practice tasks

1. Record a five-minute unscripted monologue on a random topic, then listen for the five markers above. Count them.
2. Have a native friend rate you 1–5 on each of the five components. Ask them to be unkind; explain why you are asking.
3. Book one phone call with a stranger — a restaurant, a bank, a government office — where the only information is your voice.

### Module completion gate

- A written self-assessment and a native assessment of all five components, with numbers.
- Fewer than five textbook-completeness markers in five minutes of recorded speech.
- One phone call completed where the other party did not identify you as foreign.

## 17.2 — The sound of real speech: reduction, elision, and speed

**Objective.** Learn what Koreans actually say, which is not what the textbook prints.

### Module 17.2.1 — The reduction table

Written Korean and spoken Korean differ systematically. These are the highest-frequency reductions, and using them is what makes speech sound natural.

| Written | Spoken | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `그것은` | `그건` | contraction |
| `무엇을` | `뭘` | contraction |
| `그러면` | `그럼` | contraction |
| `그렇지만` | `그치만` | very common |
| `그러니까` | `그니까` | discourse marker, almost a filler |
| `아니에요` | `아녜요` | contraction |
| `-것 같아요` | `-거 같아요` | the `ㅅ` drops |
| `했어요` | `했어요` → often `핬어요`-adjacent blur | the `ㅆ` softens between vowels |
| `왜 그러세요?` | `왜 그러세요?` → `왜 그러셨어요?` blur | phrase-level |
| `어디예요?` | `어뎌요?` | vowel compression |
| `뭐 하고 있어?` | `뭐 해?` | verb drop, meaning preserved |
| `-는다고 했잖아` | `-ㄴ댔잖아` | stacked contraction |
| `그게 무슨 말이야` | `그게 무슨` | trailing off |

### Module 17.2.2 — The elision rules

| Context | What disappears | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Between vowels | `ㅎ` weakens to nothing | `좋아` → `조아`, `많아` → `마나` |
| Before `ㄴ` / `ㅁ` | `ㄱ` `ㄷ` `ㅂ` nasalise | `국물` → `궁물`, `받는` → `반는` |
| Word-final before a vowel | the final consonant moves | `밥을` → `바블` |
| `-ㄹ` before `ㄴ` | becomes `ㄴ` | `알는` → `안는` |
| Fast speech | syllables collapse | `그러면 안 돼` → `그럼 안 돼` → `그럼 안 댜` |

> **Why this matters more than it looks:** a learner who speaks with textbook clarity sounds *careful*. Careful is the signal. Koreans reduce because the information is redundant; if you do not reduce, you sound like you are reading aloud.

### Module 17.2.3 — The rhythm

Korean is a **phrase-timed** language with a light pitch accent that is not lexical, plus a final contour that carries politeness.

| Feature | What it does | The learner error |
|---|---|---|
| Final contour | rising = question or softening; falling = statement or finality | flat endings sound cold or robotic |
| Phrase grouping | breath groups are 4–7 syllables | running sentences together without breath |
| Pitch reset | each phrase resets high, then descends | monotonous descent across a whole sentence |
| Lengthening | the final syllable of a turn is stretched for politeness | clipped endings sound abrupt |

**The drill that works:** shadow a news anchor for five minutes, then a drama scene, then a variety show. The three use different rhythms, and the third is what conversation actually sounds like.

### Practice tasks

1. Transcribe 60 seconds of a Korean variety show verbatim — including the reductions — then read your transcription aloud and compare with the audio.
2. Shadow (speak simultaneously, not repeat after) five minutes of audio daily for a month. This is the single highest-yield drill in this level.
3. Record yourself saying the same sentence five ways: flat, rising, with phrase breaks, fast, and warm. Have a native rank them.

### Module completion gate

- Shadowing 5 minutes a day for 30 consecutive days, recorded.
- A native listener identifies your speech as "natural" rather than "clear" on a blind listen.
- You produce the twelve reductions in the table above without thinking.

## 17.3 — Humour

**Objective.** Learn the one thing that separates fluent from foreign more reliably than grammar: being funny on purpose, and understanding the joke before it lands.

### Module 17.3.1 — The four engines of Korean humour

| Engine | How it works | Example type |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation + exaggeration** | the familiar is inflated until it breaks | every workplace sketch, most sitcoms |
| **Self-deprecation** | you lower yourself to raise the room | very common in group settings, and a social obligation |
| **Wordplay** | sound-based puns, often terrible | 아재개그, which is a whole genre of deliberately bad puns |
| **Reaction** | the joke is in the response, not the setup | variety shows are built on reaction, not material |

### Module 17.3.2 — 아재개그, and why bad puns matter

아재개그 is a genre of pun so weak it becomes funny through the telling. It is a social ritual: the teller knows it is bad, the listener groans, and the groan is the point.

| Pun | The mechanism |
|---|---|
| `세상에서 가장 뜨거운 과일은?` → `천도복숭아` | `천 도` = 1000 degrees |
| `소가 웃으면?` → `우하하` | `우` = cow |
| `왕이 넘어지면?` → `킹콩` | king + 콩 (falling sound) |

**Why you should learn three of them:** not because they are funny, but because deploying one is a recognised move in Korean social humour. It signals that you are *inside* the game rather than observing it. A foreigner who tells a bad 아재개그 and waits for the groan has made a cultural joke about the culture, which is a different and better joke than a good pun.

### Module 17.3.3 — The timing rules

| Rule | Korean practice |
|---|---|
| Build-up length | short — two clauses at most |
| The pause | before the punch, not after |
| The reaction | the teller reacts *with* the room; deadpan is less common than shared laughter |
| Failure | you must recover fast, and self-deprecatingly |
| Who can joke | seniority matters — a junior jokes *at* themselves, not at the senior |

> **The social constraint that surprises learners:** humour in Korean workplaces is often used to *soften a request or a criticism*, not to entertain. Reading a joke as entertainment when it is actually an instruction is a real professional hazard.

### Module 17.3.4 — Understanding without translating

The hardest part is not producing jokes; it is laughing at the right moment. Korean humour is often carried by **tone and timing rather than content** — the same sentence is funny or serious depending on the lengthening of the final syllable.

**The habit:** when a group laughs and you did not, do not ask "what did they say". Ask "what was the *turn*". The joke is usually in the last three syllables, said slowly.

### Practice tasks

1. Watch one episode of a Korean variety show with Korean subtitles, then again with none. Note which jokes you missed and why: vocabulary, reference, or timing.
2. Learn three 아재개그 and deploy one in a real conversation. Record the reaction honestly.
3. Keep a "joke log": ten things that made a Korean laugh in your presence, and what the mechanism was.

### Module completion gate

- You have made a Korean laugh at something you constructed, not repeated.
- You can name the mechanism of ten jokes you heard.
- You deferred a joke and it landed on the second attempt — and you know what changed.

## 17.4 — Subtext, 눈치, and the unsaid

**Objective.** Learn to hear the sentence under the sentence. This is the most consequential skill in the level for anyone working in Korean.

### Module 17.4.1 — Why Korean says less directly

Underlying logic: the relationship survives the message. Direct refusal, direct criticism, and direct disagreement all risk the relationship, so they are routed through implication. This is not dishonesty — it is a different contract about what a sentence is *for*.

**Consequence:** the literal content of a Korean sentence and its social content can be opposite, and the listener is expected to resolve the difference.

### Module 17.4.2 — The refusal ladder

Five ways to say no, in ascending order of softness. All of them are no.

| Phrase | Literal | What it means | How firm |
|---|---|---|---|
| `안 됩니다` | it is not possible | no | firm, formal, final |
| `좀 어렵습니다` | it is a little difficult | no | standard business no |
| `검토해 보겠습니다` | I will review it | probably no | soft, deferential |
| `생각해 볼게요` | I will think about it | no, but politely | very soft, often final |
| `일단 알아보고 연락드릴게요` | I will look into it and contact you | no, and there will be no contact | the softest no that exists |

> **The trap:** `긍정적으로 검토하겠습니다` sounds like a yes and is not. It means the matter is alive, not that it is approved. Learners who treat it as approval and then act on it do real professional damage.

### Module 17.4.3 — Reading the other signals

| Signal | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| **Silence after your proposal** | discomfort — someone senior disagrees and will not say so |
| **`그건 좀...` then a stop** | disagreement; the sentence is deliberately unfinished |
| **`그럴 수도 있겠네요`** | "I think you're wrong, and I will not argue" |
| **`제가 잘 몰라서요`** | "I don't want to be the one who says no" |
| **A question about process** | a question about your decision — `절차는 어떻게 되나요?` can mean "this was wrong" |
| **Repeating your sentence back** | they are giving you a chance to correct yourself |
| **Sudden politeness increase** | you have crossed a line, and they are re-establishing distance |

### Module 17.4.4 — Producing subtext

You must send as well as receive. The three moves:

| Need | Say | Not |
|---|---|---|
| Decline an invitation | `그날은 좀 어려울 것 같아요. 다음에 꼭 모셔 주세요.` | `안 갈래요` |
| Disagree with a senior | `말씀하신 부분도 맞는데, 혹시 이 부분은 어떻게 볼까요?` | `그건 틀렸습니다` |
| Ask for something awkward | `혹시 시간 되실 때 잠깐 뵐 수 있을까요?` | `지금 얘기 좀 해요` |

### Practice tasks

1. Take ten Korean refusals from dramas, emails, or real life and classify them on the ladder.
2. In your next three conversations, deliberately use a rung-3 or rung-4 refusal and observe whether it was understood.
3. Ask a Korean friend, after a meeting, "what did they actually mean by X". Compare with your reading. Do this ten times.

### Module completion gate

- You correctly classify ten refusals, verified by a native.
- You have declined something using a soft form and got the outcome you wanted.
- You can state, in Korean, what `그럴 수도 있겠네요` means, without hedging.

## 17.5 — Register switching at speed

**Objective.** Move between 합니다체, 해요체, 반말, and 해라체 inside one conversation, the way natives do, without dropping a level.

### Module 17.5.1 — The switch points

Natives switch constantly. The rule is not "one register per relationship" but "one register per *moment*".

| Moment | Switch |
|---|---|
| Meeting starts | 합니다체, even between close colleagues, if a client is present |
| Client leaves | 해요체 within one sentence |
| A peer becomes a friend | 반말, usually by explicit offer: `말 놓으세요` |
| A senior joins the group | 합니다체 returns, and everyone follows |
| The topic turns serious | register rises even with friends |
| You are angry | register frequently *drops* to 반말, which is the insult |
| Public speaking | 해라체 or 합니다체, never 해요체 |

### Module 17.5.2 — The offer and the acceptance

The transition to 반말 is a ritual, not a drift.

| Move | Korean |
|---|---|
| Junior asks | `말씀 놓으셔도 됩니다` / `편하게 하세요` |
| Senior offers | `우리 말 놓을까요?` / `말 편하게 해` |
| Junior accepts | `네, 그럼 편하게 하겠습니다` |
| Senior declines for now | `아직은 좀 그래요` — not an insult, a boundary |

> **The error that matters:** a learner who drifts into 반말 because a senior used it once. The senior's 반말 is an offer, not an instruction; the junior waits for explicit permission or mirrors only after it is given — and even then, in a group with an older member present, the junior may hold 해요체.

### Module 17.5.3 — The mid-sentence change

The hardest thing to learn and the clearest native marker: honouring one person while addressing another in the same sentence.

| Situation | Sentence |
|---|---|
| To a peer, about a senior | `과장님이 아까 말씀하셨는데, 우리도 준비하는 게 좋을 것 같아.` |
| To a senior, about a peer | `민준 씨가 확인한다고 했습니다.` — no `-시-`, because the subject is not honoured |
| Mixed audience | honour the verb, neutralise the noun: `관계자분들께서 확인해 주셨습니다.` |

**The rule:** `-시-` follows the *subject*, not the listener. Honouring the wrong person is a louder error than using the wrong ending.

### Module 17.5.4 — The two traps

| Trap | What happens |
|---|---|
| **The delayed switch** | you keep 합니다체 with a friend after they have switched; it reads as coldness or distance |
| **The premature switch** | you use 반말 before the offer; it reads as disrespect, and no Korean will correct you to your face |

### Practice tasks

1. Script a 90-second scene: you greet a client in 합니다체, turn to a colleague in 해요체, and answer your phone to a friend in 반말. Perform it, record it.
2. In one real day, note every register switch you observed — yours and others'. Aim for ten.
3. Have a native read a paragraph of your writing, then ask you to say the same content to three different people. Compare the three.

### Module completion gate

- A recorded 90-second performance with three correct switches and zero drops.
- Ten observed switches logged, with the trigger for each.
- You can explain why `-시-` follows the subject and not the listener.

## 17.6 — Slang, youth language, and the decay curve

**Objective.** Learn the layer of vocabulary that is real, current, and expires — and learn to handle its expiry.

### Module 17.6.1 — The honesty about slang

Slang is the highest-risk vocabulary in the language for a foreign learner, because **getting it slightly wrong is worse than not using it at all.** A Korean who uses last year's slang to a twenty-year-old sounds like a visiting relative. A foreign learner who does it sounds like a textbook that has been taught by an older textbook.

**The rule:** learn to *understand* fifty slang terms and *use* five. Comprehension is safe; production is a minefield.

### Module 17.6.2 — The current layer

These are recent, widely understood, and mostly stable enough to be recognisable for a while. Glosses are functional, not dictionary definitions.

| Term | Gloss | Register |
|---|---|---|
| `갓생` | living a disciplined, productive life ("god-life") | playful, self-improvement discourse |
| `소확행` | small but certain happiness | gentle, slightly earnest |
| `케바케` | case by case | casual |
| `쌉가능` | entirely possible | emphatic, young |
| `빼박` | no way out; certain | emphatic, young |
| `억까` | unreasonable criticism | fandom and internet |
| `혼밥` / `혼술` | eating / drinking alone | neutral, widely used |
| `플렉스` | conspicuous spending to show off | playful |
| `멘붕` | mental collapse from shock | casual, slightly dated |
| `심쿵` | heart-flutter | slightly dated, affectionate |
| `워라밸` | work-life balance | workplace, neutral |
| `N잡러` | person with several jobs | workplace, neutral |
| `TMI` | too much information; oversharing | casual |
| `아재개그` | dad joke | affectionate, slightly dated |

### Module 17.6.3 — The three-generation test

Before you use any slang term, ask three questions:

| Question | If the answer is no |
|---|---|
| Would a 25-year-old use it without irony? | it is dated — understand it, don't produce it |
| Would a 45-year-old understand it? | it is too niche for general use |
| Would you use it in front of a client? | it is not for work |

**The professional rule:** in any setting where money or hierarchy is involved, use no slang. `워라밸` and `N잡러` are borderline-acceptable workplace vocabulary; `쌉가능` is not.

### Module 17.6.4 — The decay curve

Slang decays at roughly five to seven years; some terms persist as "older person's slang", which is worse than disappearing.

| Layer | Lifetime | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ephemeral** | 2–3 years | fandom-specific abbreviations |
| **Generational** | 5–10 years | `멘붕`, `심쿵` |
| **Stabilised** | ongoing | `혼밥`, `워라밸` |
| **Lexicalised** | permanent | `눈치`, `정` — these are not slang, they are Korean |

**Maintenance:** refresh your comprehension list twice a year from actual media, and never let your productive slang be older than two years.

### Practice tasks

1. Watch one episode of a current Korean variety show and list every term you did not know. Add ten per month to a comprehension-only deck.
2. Ask three Koreans of different ages whether they would use each of the fourteen terms above. Build the generational map yourself.
3. Write a 200-word dialogue between two 22-year-olds and have a 22-year-old mark it.

### Module completion gate

- Fifty slang terms understood, five actively used, and a written note on what each of the five is safe for.
- A three-generation check performed on ten terms with real informants.
- You have never used slang in a professional email.

## 17.7 — Shared reference: the culture behind the words

**Objective.** Close the gap that vocabulary lists cannot: everything a Korean knows without being told.

### Module 17.7.1 — Why reference is harder than vocabulary

A Korean sentence can contain a reference that carries half the meaning and none of the information. `요즘 애들은` presupposes a theory of generations. `우리 때는` presupposes a shared past. Neither can be looked up, because neither is a definition.

### Module 17.7.2 — The generational shorthand

Koreans use generational labels as arguments. Each one carries a claim about who is responsible for what.

| Label | Refers to | The claim it makes |
|---|---|---|
| `386세대` | those born in the 1960s, politically active in the 1980s | the generation that democratised; now the establishment |
| `88만원 세대` | the cohort facing precarious work after 2008 | structural betrayal, not individual failure |
| `삼포` / `오포` 세대 | those who have given up three or five things (dating, marriage, children, home, hope) | diagnosis of cost |
| `MZ세대` | a blended millennial + Gen Z label | contested; many Koreans reject it as a media invention |
| `N포 세대` | the open-ended version: giving up N things | the flexible, ironic form |

> **How to handle these:** use them to *read*, not to *speak*. Saying `MZ세대는...` in a room of people in their twenties is a known way to sound like a consultant. Saying it in an essay about workplace conflict is normal.

### Module 17.7.3 — The historical floor

Every educated Korean has these as background. You do not need depth; you need recognition and the correct emotional register.

| Reference | What it is | Register when mentioned |
|---|---|---|
| `6·25` | the Korean War | grief, division, family separation |
| `5·18` | the Gwangju Democratisation Movement, 1980 | solemn; denial is politically loaded |
| `IMF` (외환위기) | the 1997 financial crisis | collective trauma; `IMF 때` is a unit of hardship |
| `분단` | national division | unresolved; avoid casual opinion |
| `한강의 기적` | the Han River Miracle, rapid industrialisation | pride, with an undertow of cost |
| `서연` / `SKY` | Seoul National, Korea, Yonsei | educational hierarchy shorthand |
| `수능` | the university entrance exam | national event; everyone has an opinion |
| `추석` / `설` | the two major holidays | family obligation, traffic, and food |

### Module 17.7.4 — The media floor

You cannot share a generation's television, but you can share its *current* references. The rule is topical, not historical: know what is on air now.

| Domain | What to maintain |
|---|---|
| Drama | the two or three currently discussed shows |
| Variety | one long-running show and one current hit |
| News | the front page of two papers, daily, including the comment section's tone |
| Sports | one sport, with the current national storyline |
| Food | the current trend — what people queue for this year |

### Practice tasks

1. Read one editorial and one entertainment headline daily for a month. Note every reference you had to look up; that list is your syllabus.
2. Ask a Korean friend to explain `MZ세대` and their opinion of the term. The opinion is the useful part.
3. Watch one episode of a currently airing drama with Korean subtitles and no dictionary, and write down every reference you missed.

### Module completion gate

- You can explain six generational labels and why each is contested.
- You can name the historical references above and the emotional register each requires.
- You have a daily news habit in Korean that has survived a month.

## 17.8 — The long monologue: speaking for an hour

**Objective.** Speak for sixty minutes on a topic you did not prepare, with structure, without running out of things to say, and without a visible search for grammar.

### Module 17.8.1 — Why an hour is a different skill

Five minutes is a set piece. Sixty minutes is a *structure*. The failure mode is not grammar — it is running dry at minute twenty-two and then repeating yourself for forty minutes.

**The arithmetic:** at a normal speaking rate, sixty minutes is roughly 9,000–12,000 Korean syllables. You need enough *content*, not enough *words*.

### Module 17.8.2 — The four-tank structure

A long talk runs on four tanks, drawn in order, and each one refills the last.

| Tank | Contents | Runs for |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The claim** | what you think and why in one sentence | 5 min |
| **2. The cases** | three concrete examples, told as stories | 20 min |
| **3. The objections** | the strongest counter-arguments, answered | 15 min |
| **4. The implications** | what follows if you are right | 15 min |
| **Refill** | returning to tank 2 with a new example | the rest |

**The refill trick:** every time you feel the tank emptying, return to a *story*, not to abstraction. Stories are inexhaustible because detail is infinite. Abstractions are finite and you will hit the wall.

### Module 17.8.3 — The discourse scaffolding

What holds an hour together is not vocabulary but signposting. Have these automatic.

| Function | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Opening | `오늘 말씀드릴 것은 크게 세 가지입니다` |
| Moving on | `다음으로`, `여기서 한 가지 짚고 넘어가면` |
| Going deeper | `조금 더 구체적으로 말씀드리면` |
| Coming back | `아까 말씀드린 것으로 돌아가서` |
| Summarising | `정리하면`, `결국 핵심은` |
| Buying time | `잠깐 생각을 정리해 보면`, `쉽게 말해서` |

> **Buying time is a skill, not a failure.** `잠깐 생각을 정리해 보면` said calmly reads as deliberation. Silence with a searching expression reads as a vocabulary gap. The difference is entirely in the phrase.

### Module 17.8.4 — The stamina build

| Week | Target | Topic source |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | 10 minutes | something you know well |
| 3–4 | 20 minutes | a news topic, 5 minutes' prep |
| 5–6 | 30 minutes | a random prompt, no prep |
| 7–8 | 45 minutes | a random prompt, recorded, one listener |
| 9–12 | 60 minutes | no prep, two listeners, questions at the end |

**Record all of them.** Listen at 1× for grammar, and at 1× a second time for content — you will find that the grammar is fine and the content is thin, which is the actual problem to fix.

### Practice tasks

1. Do the twelve-week build. Do not skip the middle; the 30-minute wall is where the skill is made.
2. After each recording, count filler words per minute. Target under five.
3. Have a listener interrupt at three random points and ask a question. Resume without losing the thread.

### Module completion gate

- Three recorded monologues of 45 minutes or longer.
- One 60-minute talk with two listeners and live questions.
- Filler rate under five per minute across a full recording.

## 17.9 — Writing a short story in Korean

**Objective.** Produce fiction that a Korean would read to the end. This is the last item in the roadmap's mastery definition, and it is the one that most learners skip.

### Module 17.9.1 — Why fiction is the hardest test

Non-fiction lets you use the registers you have drilled. Fiction requires **voice** — a narrator whose sentences are consistent, whose omissions are deliberate, and whose dialogue sounds like people rather than like examples.

It also requires the thing that Korean short fiction does better than almost any other tradition: **restraint**. The Korean short story is often built around what is not said, and around a situation that does not resolve.

### Module 17.9.2 — The Korean short-story shape

| Element | The convention |
|---|---|
| **Opening** | a scene, not a thesis — the first sentence places you in a room |
| **Development** | an ordinary situation with one pressure applied to it |
| **The turn** | a small fracture, not a plot twist — often one line of dialogue |
| **Ending** | open. The story stops; it does not conclude. Meaning is left with the reader |
| **Length** | 3,000–8,000 characters is the classic magazine range |

> **The most common foreign mistake:** resolving the story. Korean short fiction frequently ends *before* the resolution, on an image. If your ending explains what it meant, cut it.

### Module 17.9.3 — Voice and viewpoint

| Choice | Effect | When |
|---|---|---|
| **1인칭** (`나`) | intimacy; unreliability is available | the story turns on what the narrator cannot see |
| **3인칭 제한** | distance with access to one mind | the classic modern Korean mode |
| **전지적 시점** | omniscience | older fiction; hard to sustain without sounding dated |
| **Free indirect speech** | the narrator absorbs the character's voice | the hardest and the most modern — see L14 §14.4 |

### Module 17.9.4 — Dialogue that sounds like speech

Four rules, learned from reading rather than from grammar.

| Rule | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Endings carry relationship** | the same line in 반말 and 해요체 is a different scene |
| **People do not answer the question** | real dialogue deflects; a direct answer reads as an exam |
| **Use `-는데` to end turns** | trailing off is how Korean dialogue avoids closure |
| **Cut the stage directions** | `그가 말했다` is rarer in Korean fiction than in English; the line-break does the work |

### Module 17.9.5 — The build

| Stage | Task |
|---|---|
| 1 | Transcribe a page of dialogue from a Korean novel you admire. Count the speech tags. |
| 2 | Write a 500-word scene with two characters and no plot. |
| 3 | Write 1,500 words with one pressure and no resolution. |
| 4 | Write 3,000 words with a turn. |
| 5 | Have a Korean writer mark it for voice, not grammar. |

### Practice tasks

1. Read 현진건 「운수 좋은 날」 and 이효석 「메밀꽃 필 무렵」, then 윤흥길 「장마」. Note how each ends.
2. Write one scene per week for eight weeks, each 500–800 words, each with a different viewpoint.
3. Submit one story to a Korean writing group or a language-exchange critique circle. Take the criticism on structure, not on particles.

### Module completion gate

- A 3,000-character story with a turn, an open ending, and dialogue a Korean would believe.
- One native reader finishes it without stopping, and says so.
- You can name the viewpoint and the voice strategy of your own story.

## 17.10 — Reading at native speed, and the invisible error

**Objective.** Two residual gaps: reading as fast as you think, and writing that leaves no trace.

### Module 17.10.1 — The reading speeds

| Material | Native speed | Your target |
|---|---|---|
| News article | 400–500 characters/min | 350+ |
| Editorial | 300–400 | 250+ |
| Novel | 300–500 | 250+ |
| Academic paper | 150–250 | 120+ |
| Contract or legal text | 80–150 | 60+ |

**Why speed matters beyond convenience:** below a threshold, reading is decoding and comprehension collapses. Above it, reading becomes *thinking*, and you can disagree with a text while reading it — which is the point of being literate in a language.

### Module 17.10.2 — The three reading modes

| Mode | Use | Method |
|---|---|---|
| **Skim** | do I need this? | title, first sentence of each paragraph, last paragraph |
| **Scan** | find one thing | eyes on the left edge, hunting the keyword |
| **Read** | comprehension or pleasure | 200-character chunks, no re-reading |

**The rule that builds speed:** never re-read a sentence. If you missed it, keep going — the redundancy in real prose will recover the meaning. Re-reading is the habit that keeps learners at 120 characters/min forever.

### Module 17.10.3 — The invisible error

At this level, your errors stop being grammatical and become *slightly off* — the sentence a Korean would not have written, with nothing wrong with it.

| Type | Example | Why a native flinches |
|---|---|---|
| **Collocation** | `회의를 만들다` | it is `회의를 잡다` or `소집하다` |
| **Register bleed** | `-습니다` in a 해라체 essay | breaks the texture |
| **Over-explicit connector** | `그러나` in casual prose | sounds like a translated essay |
| **Translationese rhythm** | long noun phrase + passive | reads as machine output |
| **Particle decoration** | `-은/는` used for emphasis where it marks topic | the information structure is wrong |

**The only reliable detector is a native reader.** Your own ear cannot hear these, which is why L18's teaching work is the real fix: explaining the language is how you finally see it.

### Practice tasks

1. Time yourself on five articles a week for a month. Record words per minute and comprehension after.
2. Write 500 words of opinion prose and have a native mark every sentence they would rewrite, with a reason. Categorise the reasons using the table above.
3. Read one novel in Korean with no dictionary, at speed, and accept 85% comprehension.

### Module completion gate

- 350 characters/min on news with ≥ 85% comprehension, measured.
- Twenty labelled invisible errors, categorised, with fixes.
- One novel finished without a dictionary.

## 17.11 — The accent question, answered honestly

**Objective.** Settle the thing every learner asks and almost nobody answers straight.

### Module 17.11.1 — The five features of a foreign accent

| Feature | Fixable? | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Vowel quality** (`ㅓ` vs `ㅗ`, `ㅡ` vs `ㅜ`) | mostly | minimal-pair drills, spectrogram feedback, a coach |
| **Final consonants** unreleased or over-released | yes | drill the unreleased stop; Korean stops are not aspirated finally |
| **Tensification** (`ㄲ` `ㄸ` `ㅃ` `ㅆ` `ㅉ`) missing | yes | minimal pairs with a native, 20 reps each |
| **Intonation contour** | yes | shadowing, 3 months |
| **Voice quality and resonance** | rarely | this is where "sounds foreign" mostly lives, and it is the least fixable |

### Module 17.11.2 — The honest ranking

| Goal | Realistic? |
|---|---|
| Comprehensible, natural, pleasant accent | yes, for almost everyone |
| No identifiable accent on short utterances | yes, with 6–12 months of focused prosody work |
| Mistaken for native on the phone, consistently | yes for some, no for others, and no amount of work guarantees it |
| Mistaken for native in a long conversation | depends more on 17.1–17.7 than on pronunciation |

> **The thing to internalise:** accent is the *last* thing Koreans use to decide you are foreign. Rhythm, reference, and subtext come first. A learner with a slight accent who handles 눈치 perfectly is treated as a fluent speaker; a learner with perfect vowels who misses a refusal is treated as a foreigner who speaks well.

### Module 17.11.3 — The 12-week prosody programme

| Weeks | Focus | Daily |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | shadowing, 10 min | news anchor, then drama |
| 5–8 | pitch contour, 10 min | record and compare waveforms |
| 9–10 | phrase-final lengthening | politeness contours |
| 11–12 | free speech with a coach | 30 min weekly, targeted corrections only |

### Practice tasks

1. Record the same paragraph at the start and the end of twelve weeks. Play both to a native, blind, and ask which sounds more natural.
2. Do minimal-pair drills for the five tense consonants, 20 reps each, recorded.
3. Have a coach identify your top two features and ignore the rest for twelve weeks.

### Module completion gate

- A before-and-after recording, judged by a blind native listener.
- Two features identified, worked on, and measurably improved.
- A written statement of what you have decided not to fix, and why.

# LEVEL 18 — TEACHING AND LIFELONG MASTERY

> **Goal.** Two things. First, be able to *explain* Korean — which is the deepest test of understanding there is, and the only one that exposes the gaps that fluency hides. Second, build the regime that keeps the language alive for decades, because the single most common outcome of reaching an advanced level is losing it by neglect.
>
> **Why teaching is the final level.** A user of a language can be excellent and still not know why anything works. A teacher must be able to say what the rule is, when it bends, which exceptions matter, and why the learner's version was wrong rather than merely different. That knowledge cannot be faked, and building it fixes things fluency never touched.

## Level dashboard

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Level** | 18 — Teaching and Lifelong Mastery |
| **Difficulty** | Mastery |
| **Suggested duration** | 3–6 months to the teaching certification; the maintenance regime is permanent |
| **Study commitment** | 2 hours per day, of which at least one hour is teaching or preparing to teach |
| **Core tools** | One or two real learners, a grammar reference (국립국어원, 한국어 문법 사전), a rubric you built, a recorder |
| **Main project** | **`the-teacher`** — a taught unit, a diagnosis, a curriculum, and a rubric |
| **Final outcome** | You taught a 60-minute lesson that worked, diagnosed a learner's error pattern, and can explain any structure on demand. |

**Prerequisites.** Level 17 complete.

## 18.1 — What teaching demands that using does not

**Objective.** Understand the four capabilities that separate a fluent user from a teacher, and accept that you probably have only two of them.

### Module 18.1.1 — The four capabilities

| Capability | The user has it | The teacher needs it |
|---|---|---|
| **Use** | you produce correct Korean | — |
| **Analysis** | — | you can name the structure and its constraints |
| **Diagnosis** | — | you can tell *why* a learner produced this error |
| **Sequencing** | — | you know which order a learner should meet things in |

> **The asymmetry that matters:** you learned Korean in an order nobody would design. You met `-는데` in a drama, `-더라` in a song, and the honorific system in a workplace. A teacher has to construct a better order than the one they lived through — and that requires knowing the dependencies, not just the facts.

### Module 18.1.2 — The three questions you must be able to answer

1. **What is the rule?** (form and meaning)
2. **When does it not apply?** (constraints, register, exceptions)
3. **Why did the learner get it wrong?** (diagnosis)

If you can only answer question one, you are a native speaker with a textbook, which is not a teacher.

### Module 18.1.3 — The humility clause

You will be worse than a native Korean teacher at explaining Korean culture and worse than a trained teacher at classroom management. What you have that neither has: **you did this recently, consciously, and from outside** — which means you remember what it was like not to know, and you know which explanations actually worked on you.

**Teach from that.** Your comparative advantage is not authority. It is the memory of confusion.

### Practice tasks

1. Take three structures you use automatically (`-는데`, `-더라`, `-시-`). Write the rule, the constraints, and three exceptions for each, without looking anything up. Then check yourself.
2. Find one learner and ask them to explain a grammar point they know well. Note what they cannot explain.
3. Write your "confusion memories": the five things that took you longest to understand, and what finally made them click.

### Module completion gate

- Three structures documented with rule, constraints, and exceptions.
- Five confusion memories written, each with the resolution.
- You can state in one sentence what your comparative advantage is as a teacher.

## 18.2 — The metalanguage: saying how Korean works

**Objective.** Learn the terminology, because you cannot teach what you cannot name.

### Module 18.2.1 — Why natives cannot do this

A Korean says `-시-` correctly and has no idea it is called 주체 높임 선어말어미. That is not ignorance — it is the normal condition of a native speaker. Explicit knowledge and implicit ability are stored separately, which is why every Korean teacher training programme teaches grammar that the trainees already use perfectly.

### Module 18.2.2 — The core terminology

| Term | English | What it names | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **품사** | part of speech | noun, verb, adjective, adverb, particle | `먹다` (동사) |
| **어간 / 어미** | stem / ending | the invariant part, the inflecting part | `먹-` + `-는다` |
| **선어말어미** | pre-final ending | honorific, tense, aspect, mood | `-시-`, `-았/었-`, `-겠-` |
| **어말어미** | final ending | the sentence-type ending | `-습니다`, `-어요`, `-다`, `-자` |
| **불규칙 활용** | irregular conjugation | the seven irregular classes | `듣다` → `들어` (ㄷ irregular) |
| **높임법** | honorific system | subject, object, addressee | `-시-`, `드리다`, `-습니다` |
| **피동 / 사동** | passive / causative | `-이- 히- 리- 기-`, `-게 하다` | `보이다`, `먹이다` |
| **의존명사** | bound noun | a noun that cannot stand alone | `것`, `수`, `줄`, `데`, `바`, `지` |
| **보조용언** | auxiliary verb | a second verb carrying aspect | `-어 버리다`, `-고 있다` |
| **조사** | particle | case and topic markers | `이/가`, `은/는`, `을/를` |
| **호응** | agreement / collocation | a form that requires a partner | `-에 대해` + `생각하다` |

### Module 18.2.3 — The seven irregular classes

Every learner hits these, and every learner needs them explained in one page, not thirty.

| Class | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **ㅂ** | `ㅂ` → `우` before a vowel | `춥다` → `추워요`; but `좁다` → `좁아요` (regular) |
| **ㄷ** | `ㄷ` → `ㄹ` before a vowel | `듣다` → `들어요`; but `받다` → `받아요` (regular) |
| **ㅅ** | `ㅅ` drops before a vowel | `낫다` → `나아요` |
| **ㅎ** | `ㅎ` drops, vowels contract | `하얗다` → `하얘요` |
| **르** | `르` → `ㄹㄹ` | `모르다` → `몰라요` |
| **러** | `르` → `ㄹ` + `러` | `이르다` → `일러요` |
| **우** | `우` drops before a vowel | `푸다` → `퍼요` |

> **The teaching point learners never get:** the irregular classes are not lists to memorise. They are historical sound changes that applied to a *subset* of verbs, which is why `춥다` is irregular and `좁다` is not. Explaining that turns thirty memorised items into one rule with exceptions — which is what a teacher does and a textbook does not.

### Module 18.2.4 — The four honorific systems

| System | What it honours | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **주체 높임** | the subject | `-시-` |
| **객체 높임** | the object | `드리다`, `모시다`, `뵙다`, `여쭈다` |
| **상대 높임** | the listener | `-습니다` / `-어요` / `-다` / `-네` |
| **특수 어휘** | the person referred to | `진지`, `연세`, `댁`, `성함` |

**The classic teaching error:** presenting honorifics as one system. They are four, they operate independently, and a sentence can honour the subject and insult the listener at the same time — which is exactly what a learner does when they use `-시-` with 반말.

### Practice tasks

1. Write a one-page grammar sheet for three structures, using the terminology above, and test it on a real learner.
2. Take a paragraph of your own Korean and label every ending as 선어말 or 어말.
3. Explain the ㅂ irregular class in under 90 seconds, out loud, without notes.

### Module completion gate

- The eleven terms defined from memory, with examples.
- The seven irregular classes explained with the reason, not just the rule.
- One learner understood your explanation on the first attempt.

## 18.3 — Diagnosing error

**Objective.** Move from "that's wrong" to "that's wrong, and here is the mechanism".

### Module 18.3.1 — The error taxonomy

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **전이 오류** transfer | the L1 pattern applied to Korean | English speaker: `나는 행복한이다` from "I am happy" |
| **발달 오류** developmental | a correct rule over-applied | `먹었습니다` → `먹었어습니다` |
| **화석화** fossilisation | an error that has stopped responding to correction | `-는데` used as a universal connector |
| **회피** avoidance | the learner avoids the structure entirely | never uses causatives |
| **과잉일반화** overgeneralisation | an irregular treated as regular | `듣었어요` instead of `들었어요` |

### Module 18.3.2 — The three-question diagnosis

| Question | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Is it systematic or a slip?** | if it disappears when they slow down, it is performance; if it survives, it is competence |
| **Does it match their first language?** | if yes, it is transfer — and the fix is contrastive |
| **Is it in a structure they avoid elsewhere?** | if yes, it is not an error but a hole; the fix is production practice, not correction |

### Module 18.3.3 — The diagnostic interview

You cannot diagnose from writing alone, because writing is edited. Sit with the learner and do three things:

1. **Elicit the structure** without naming it: "tell me about a time you almost missed a train".
2. **Ask them to self-correct**: "anything you'd change?" — this separates slips from gaps.
3. **Ask them to explain the rule**: a learner who can state the rule and still breaks it has a processing problem, not a knowledge problem, and the fix is fluency practice, not explanation.

### Module 18.3.4 — The error profile

For each learner, keep one page:

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Error | the observed form |
| Type | transfer / developmental / fossilised / avoidance |
| Frequency | per 100 words |
| Structure | which grammar point it belongs to |
| Intervention | what you will do, and when you will check |

**The rule:** work on **two** errors at a time. A learner given eight corrections fixes none of them.

### Practice tasks

1. Collect 200 words of a learner's writing and 3 minutes of their speech. Build the error profile.
2. Compare written and spoken: the difference is their *monitoring*, not their knowledge.
3. Pick the top two errors and design one activity for each.

### Module completion gate

- One complete error profile, with frequencies, for a real learner.
- Correct classification of ten errors into the taxonomy.
- Two interventions designed, and evidence that they moved the number.

## 18.4 — Correction without demoralisation

**Objective.** Learn the single most consequential teaching skill: giving feedback that produces change without producing shame.

### Module 18.4.1 — The correction ladder

Six moves, from least to most intrusive. Use the lowest rung that works.

| Rung | Move | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Recast** — say it correctly, naturally, without comment | learner: `어제 영화 봤어요?` → you: `아, 어제 영화 봤어요?` (correcting tense by echo) |
| **2** | **Clarification request** | `잘 못 들었어요. 다시 말해 줄래요?` |
| **3** | **Elicitation** — leave a gap | `어제 영화를...?` |
| **4** | **Metalinguistic clue** | `여기서는 과거를 써야 할 것 같아요` |
| **5** | **Explicit correction** | `'봤어요'가 아니라 '봤었어요'예요` |
| **6** | **Repeat the error with a rising tone** | `봤어요?` (with a querying intonation) |

**Why the ladder matters:** rungs 1–3 keep the conversation going and preserve face. Rungs 5–6 stop the flow, and a learner corrected at rung 5 five times in ten minutes will stop speaking. In a conversation class, live almost entirely on rungs 1–3 and save rung 5 for the review at the end.

### Module 18.4.2 — What to correct, and what to let go

| Correct | Let go |
|---|---|
| anything that blocks meaning | a slip the learner catches themselves |
| a *fossilising* form, consistently | a one-off error in a hard sentence |
| register errors in a professional context | accent, during a fluency activity |
| two errors per session, maximum | everything else, this session |

> **The rule of two:** two corrections per session, chosen deliberately, marked in your notes, revisited next time. A learner who fixes two things a week fixes a hundred things a year. A learner corrected on everything fixes nothing, because none of it is retained.

### Module 18.4.3 — The praise ratio

Research on corrective feedback is consistent on one point: **frequency of correction predicts uptake, and frequency of praise predicts willingness to speak.** You need both, and you need them in a ratio a learner can feel — roughly three positive or neutral interactions for every correction.

**It must be specific.** `잘했어요` is noise. `방금 '-는데'를 자연스럽게 썼어요. 그건 어려운 거예요` is information.

### Module 18.4.4 — Correcting a fossilised error

Fossilised errors need a different method, because correction has already failed.

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Name it explicitly.** "You have a habit I want to break. It will feel wrong." |
| 2 | **Make it conscious.** Drill it in isolation, 20 reps, until it feels strange. |
| 3 | **Contrast it.** Put the correct and incorrect forms side by side, ten times. |
| 4 | **Monitor it.** For two weeks, every time they produce it, you raise one finger — no words. |
| 5 | **Re-test** in free speech after a month. If it returns, the fossil is deeper than the drill. |

### Practice tasks

1. Record yourself teaching and count your rungs. Most new teachers live at rung 5 and think they are helping.
2. Take a learner's fossilised error and run the five steps over two weeks.
3. Write five specific praise statements about a real learner's work. Notice how much harder it is than generic praise.

### Module completion gate

- A recorded lesson where ≥ 80% of corrections are rungs 1–3.
- One fossilised error broken, with a before-and-after recording.
- Five specific praise statements delivered and noted.

## 18.5 — Building a curriculum

**Objective.** Design a course backwards, from outcome to lesson, instead of forwards from a textbook's chapter one.

### Module 18.5.1 — Backward design

| Step | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Outcome** | what should they be able to *do*? | order a meal, complain, and apologise, in 해요체 |
| **2. Assessment** | how will we know? | a five-minute role-play with a native |
| **3. Task** | what activity produces that? | a restaurant simulation with a problem injected |
| **4. Language** | what do they need to do the task? | food vocabulary, `-주세요`, `-ㄹ 수 있나요`, complaint formulas |
| **5. Sequence** | what must come first? | the vocabulary before the task; the task before the free role-play |

**The inversion that matters:** textbooks go language → task. Teaching goes task → language. The learner acquires the structure because they needed it five minutes ago, not because it was chapter four.

### Module 18.5.2 — The four strands

A balanced course contains all four, in roughly equal time. Omit one and the learner stalls.

| Strand | What it is | Typical share |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning-focused input** | listening and reading they can mostly understand | 25% |
| **Meaning-focused output** | speaking and writing with a real purpose | 25% |
| **Language-focused learning** | grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, deliberately studied | 25% |
| **Fluency development** | doing what they already know, faster | 25% |

**The common design failure:** a course that is 80% language-focused learning — which is what most self-study and most bad textbooks are — produces learners who know about Korean and cannot use it.

### Module 18.5.3 — The dependency order

You cannot teach everything at once, and some things must precede others.

| Must come before | Because |
|---|---|
| `-아/어요` before `-습니다` | the polite informal is the base; the formal is a substitution |
| particles before connectors | you need sentences before you can join them |
| `-고` and `-는데` before `-지만` and `-므로` | the neutral connectors are the substrate for the marked ones |
| tense before aspect | `-았/었-` before `-고 있다` |
| direct speech before reported speech | `-다고` is a transformation of something |
| honorifics before workplace register | you cannot switch registers you do not own |

### Module 18.5.4 — The lesson template

| Phase | Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm** | 5 | retrieval of last lesson, spoken, no notes |
| **Input** | 15 | a text or audio they mostly understand |
| **Notice** | 10 | they find the target structure themselves before you name it |
| **Practice** | 15 | controlled, then freer |
| **Produce** | 10 | a task that requires the structure |
| **Close** | 5 | what they can now do that they could not at the start |

**The "notice" phase is the one teachers skip and the one that matters.** A structure they noticed themselves is retained; a structure they were told about is not.

### Practice tasks

1. Design a six-lesson unit with backward design, and write the outcome first.
2. Audit the four strands in your own learning history. Which was missing, and what did it cost you?
3. Teach one lesson from the template and time each phase. Almost everyone overruns the input and cuts the production.

### Module completion gate

- One six-lesson unit, backward-designed, with the four strands balanced.
- One lesson taught from the template with all six phases present.
- A written dependency map for the first twenty structures a beginner meets.

## 18.6 — Assessment and rubrics

**Objective.** Build a rubric that measures what you actually intended to teach, and defend the score to the learner.

### Module 18.6.1 — The rubric anatomy

A usable rubric has **three or four criteria**, each with **four bands** and a **one-line descriptor per band**. More than four criteria and nobody reads it; fewer than three and it measures one thing.

| Criterion | Band 4 | Band 3 | Band 2 | Band 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Task completion** | does everything asked, plus elaboration | does everything asked | does most of it | does some of it |
| **Accuracy** | errors do not distract | occasional errors, self-corrected | frequent errors, meaning clear | errors block meaning |
| **Range** | varied structures and vocabulary | adequate variety | repetitive | minimal |
| **Register and appropriateness** | correct throughout | mostly correct | inconsistent | wrong for the context |

### Module 18.6.2 — The three rubric sins

| Sin | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Halo** | a likeable learner scores high on everything | score criteria independently, in a random order |
| **Grammar dominance** | accuracy is 60% of the mark | weight by what you taught; if the unit was about register, register dominates |
| **Unmeasurable descriptors** | "sounds natural" | state the observable: "uses three or more connectors without prompting" |

### Module 18.6.3 — Feedback that a learner can act on

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Good effort" | "You completed all four parts of the task. Your register slipped twice, both in the apology." |
| "Grammar needs work" | "Past tense in `-았/었-` is solid; `-더라` is used where `-았/었-` is needed. Drill the contrast, not the tense." |
| "8/10" | "3/4 on task, 2/4 on register, 3/4 on accuracy — the register score is what to work on next." |

### Module 18.6.4 — The progress measure

Score the same task at the start and end of a unit, with the same rubric. **The delta is the learning** — not the absolute score. A learner who moves from band 2 to band 3 on register has learned more than one who was already at band 4 and stayed there.

### Practice tasks

1. Build a four-criterion rubric for a speaking task and use it on three learners.
2. Score the same recording twice, a week apart, without looking at the first score. The difference is your own reliability.
3. Give a learner feedback in the three-column format above and ask them to tell you, in their own words, what to work on next.

### Module completion gate

- A rubric you built, used on three learners, with the scores recorded.
- Your self-reliability within one band on re-scoring.
- One learner can state their next target without you telling them twice.

## 18.7 — Pronunciation coaching

**Objective.** Fix someone else's sound, which requires hearing it more precisely than you hear your own.

### Module 18.7.1 — The diagnostic chart

Before you teach any sound, find out which contrasts the learner cannot *hear*. Production follows perception; a learner who cannot distinguish `ㅓ` from `ㅗ` will never produce them reliably.

| Contrast | The test | If they fail |
|---|---|---|
| `ㅓ` / `ㅗ` | `커피` / `코피` | perceptual training before production |
| `ㅡ` / `ㅜ` | `크다` / `쿠다` | same |
| plain / tense | `달` / `딸` | the most common Asian-L1 problem |
| plain / aspirated | `달` / `탈` | VOT training |
| final `ㄴ` / `ㅇ` | `간` / `강` | nasal assimilation drills |
| `ㄹ` initial | `라면` | the tap, not the English r |

### Module 18.7.2 — The four-step sound drill

| Step | Activity | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Hear** | discrimination task: same or different? | 3 min |
| **2. Say** | the sound in isolation, with feedback | 2 min |
| **3. Contrast** | minimal pairs, alternating | 5 min |
| **4. Use** | the sound inside a real sentence | 5 min |

**Never skip step 1.** Production drills without perceptual training produce a learner who practises the wrong target confidently.

### Module 18.7.3 — Prosody before segments

For most learners above intermediate level, the problem is not individual sounds — it is rhythm and phrase contour. Working on segments at that stage is wasted effort.

| Symptom | The real problem |
|---|---|
| "They sound like they're reading" | phrase-final contour, no lengthening |
| "They sound choppy" | no liaison across word boundaries |
| "They sound flat" | pitch reset not happening per phrase |
| "They sound rude" | falling contour on polite forms |

### Practice tasks

1. Run the diagnostic chart on two learners and record which contrasts fail.
2. Teach one sound with the four-step drill and record the before and after.
3. Take a learner with "good sounds but unnatural speech" and work only on prosody for three weeks.

### Module completion gate

- Two complete diagnostic charts.
- One sound fixed with recorded evidence.
- One learner whose prosody measurably improved in three weeks.

## 18.8 — Materials, and the four resources

**Objective.** Build the teaching kit, and know what each piece is for.

### Module 18.8.1 — The four resources

| Resource | What it is for | What it is not for |
|---|---|---|
| **A reference grammar** | answering "what is the rule" precisely | teaching a lesson |
| **A graded reader or textbook** | controlled input at the right level | authentic language |
| **Authentic material** (news, drama, signage) | motivation and real language | beginners, unless scaffolded |
| **A corpus or phrase bank** | collocation and frequency questions | grammar explanation |

### Module 18.8.2 — Authentic material, scaffolded

The mistake is either "only textbooks" (boring, unnatural) or "only authentic" (overwhelming). The fix is a **task that makes authentic text doable**.

| Scaffold | Example |
|---|---|
| **Pre-teach five words** | the five most frequent unknown words, no more |
| **Set a gist task first** | "how many people are mentioned?" before "what did they say?" |
| **Gloss, don't translate** | a Korean-language definition, or a picture |
| **Allow one pass with subtitles** | then a second without |

### Module 18.8.3 — The materials you should build yourself

| Item | Why it beats a textbook |
|---|---|
| A 200-item phrase bank for one domain | it is the learner's actual domain |
| Ten recorded dialogues at their level | they can slow them down and replay |
| An error log per learner | no textbook tracks their errors |
| A one-page grammar summary per structure | textbooks spread it over six pages |

### Practice tasks

1. Build a 200-item phrase bank for a domain you know — your own job, for example.
2. Take one authentic news clip and design three tasks around it: gist, detail, and reaction.
3. Write ten one-page grammar sheets for your five most-taught structures.

### Module completion gate

- A 200-item phrase bank, sourced and glossed.
- One authentic text with three scaffolded tasks.
- Ten one-page grammar sheets.

## 18.9 — Teaching culture without caricature

**Objective.** Handle the part of the job that is easiest to do badly, and that learners will judge you on for years.

### Module 18.9.1 — The caricature trap

The failure mode is presenting culture as rules: "Koreans always...", "you must never...". This produces learners who are anxious, who over-apply the rule in the wrong context, and who cannot read the exceptions — which are most of the cases.

| Caricature | The accurate version |
|---|---|
| "Koreans never say no" | there are five ways to say no, and the choice depends on relationship and stakes |
| "Koreans are hierarchical" | hierarchy is one axis among several, and it is negotiated, not fixed |
| "Koreans eat dog" / "Koreans are obsessed with beauty" | a stereotype with a partial fact inside it, which is the definition of a stereotype |
| "You must use two hands" | true in some contexts, optional in others, and nobody notices if you do not |

### Module 18.9.2 — The three-question method for any cultural claim

| Question | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Who does this?** | age, region, class, generation — the claim is usually narrower than stated |
| **When is it violated?** | every norm has exceptions, and the exceptions are the interesting part |
| **What does violating it cost?** | mild awkwardness, or real offence — the learner needs the scale |

### Module 18.9.3 — Teaching the exception explicitly

A learner who knows "Koreans use honorifics" will be lost the first time a boss uses 반말 to them. A learner who knows "honorifics track the *relationship*, and the relationship changes" will survive.

**The rule:** every cultural claim you teach must come with its exception, in the same lesson. A claim without an exception is a stereotype with good manners.

### Module 18.9.4 — Your outsider position

You will be asked to explain things a Korean would never have to. That is an advantage: you can explain the *comparison*, which a native cannot. Do not fake authority you do not have — say "I notice that..." rather than "Koreans believe...".

### Practice tasks

1. Take five common claims about Korean culture and run the three-question method on each.
2. Write one lesson that teaches a norm and its exception in the same 20 minutes.
3. Record yourself explaining a cultural point. Listen for the phrase "Koreans always".

### Module completion gate

- Five claims analysed, with who/when/cost.
- One lesson teaching a norm and its exception together.
- Zero instances of "Koreans always" in a recorded explanation.

## 18.10 — The decay curve, and the maintenance regime

**Objective.** Accept that mastery is not a state you reach but a level you defend, and build the defence.

### Module 18.10.1 — What decays, in what order

| Skill | Half-life without use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Productive vocabulary** (rare words) | 1–2 years | low frequency, no retrieval |
| **Speaking fluency** | 1–2 years | it is a motor and processing skill |
| **Listening to fast speech** | 2–3 years | the perception threshold is fragile |
| **Reading** | 5+ years | redundant, self-cueing, robust |
| **Pronunciation** | 10+ years | motor memory is the most durable |
| **Grammar knowledge** | very slow | declarative knowledge is stable |

> **The counter-intuitive result:** reading survives longest and speaking decays fastest, which is exactly backwards from how people maintain their languages. Almost everyone keeps reading and stops speaking, then wonders why they can no longer talk.

### Module 18.10.2 — The four floors

The maintenance regime is four floors. Miss one for a month and the building is fine. Miss one for a year and it is not.

| Floor | Minimum | What it protects |
|---|---|---|
| **Input floor** | 30 minutes a day of Korean you did not choose for study | listening and reading |
| **Output floor** | 60 minutes a week of live speech with a native | fluency |
| **Retrieval floor** | 10 minutes a day of SRS, or 20 minutes of writing | vocabulary |
| **Growth floor** | one new domain per year — a new field, a new register, a new genre | range |

**The genius of the floors:** they are small enough to survive a bad week, which is the only reason any long-term regime works.

### Module 18.10.3 — The annual cycle

| Period | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Quarter 1** | the growth floor — take on a new domain |
| **Quarter 2** | the output floor — a project that requires speech |
| **Quarter 3** | a deliberate weakness — the thing you have avoided for years |
| **Quarter 4** | consolidation — reread, re-record, compare with previous years |

### Module 18.10.4 — The measurement

Once a year, do the same four things and keep the recordings:

1. A 10-minute unscripted monologue on the same prompt.
2. A page of handwriting.
3. A cold reading of a newspaper editorial, timed.
4. A 5-minute conversation with a stranger.

**Compare year on year.** Not with a target — with yourself. This is the only longitudinal measure that means anything, and almost nobody does it.

### Practice tasks

1. Write your four floors with actual times and actual people.
2. Record the four annual measures now, and date them.
3. Identify the skill you are most likely to let decay, and write the specific defence.

### Module completion gate

- The four floors written with names, times, and a fallback for a bad week.
- Four baseline recordings, dated and stored.
- A written answer to "what will I have lost in five years if I stop".

## 18.11 — Re-mastery, and the twenty-year view

**Objective.** Learn how to come back, because you will stop at some point, and the return is a skill of its own.

### Module 18.11.1 — The re-entry sequence

When you come back after six months or three years, do not start where you left off. Start here:

| Week | Action | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Listening only.** 30 min/day, content you have already heard. | rebuilds the perception threshold fast, and it is the least threatening |
| 2 | **Speaking with a tolerant partner.** Someone who knew you before. | they remember your level and will not treat you as a beginner |
| 3 | **Reading, easy and pleasurable.** Not study material. | restores the habit, not the skill |
| 4 | **The diagnostic.** The four annual measures, compared with your last recordings. | tells you what actually decayed |

**What not to do:** restart a grammar textbook. Your declarative knowledge survived. What decayed is retrieval speed, and only use restores it.

### Module 18.11.2 — The twenty-year view

| Years | What you are doing | What you are not doing |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | building the structure | — |
| 3–6 | filling it, specialising | acquiring new grammar systematically |
| 6–10 | using it professionally, teaching it | studying it |
| 10–20 | maintaining, deepening one domain, becoming the person others ask | measuring yourself against learners |

**The shift at year six is the important one.** After that point, the language is not a project. It is a fact about you, maintained by the floors, deepened by the annual cycle, and occasionally tested by a year in which you did not use it at all.

### Module 18.11.3 — What mastery actually feels like

Not fluency fireworks. Three quieter things:

| Feeling | What it means |
|---|---|
| **You stop noticing** | you read a novel and forget you are reading Korean |
| **You notice differently** | you catch a writer's bad sentence, and can say why |
| **You can explain** | someone asks a question and you answer it in ninety seconds |

### Practice tasks

1. Write a realistic "I stopped for two years" scenario and the four-week re-entry plan for it.
2. Write your twenty-year plan in one page, with the floors and the annual cycle on it.
3. Write a 500-word answer in Korean to: `한국어를 왜 배우십니까?` — the answer you would give in year ten, not year one.

### Module completion gate

- A written re-entry protocol with a named partner and named material.
- A one-page twenty-year plan.
- One 500-word Korean essay on why you are doing this, written from the far end.

# PHASE 7 CAPSTONE — THE PASSING

> **The brief.** There is no exam above TOPIK 6, so the capstone is the invention of one: a panel of three native speakers who do not know you well, a day of performance, and a verdict you cannot appeal.
>
> **Why it is called the Passing.** Because the thing being tested is not knowledge. It is whether you can pass — as a colleague, as a conversation partner, as a writer, as a teacher — in front of people with no reason to be generous.

## A. The hour

Speak for **60 minutes**, unprepared, on a topic drawn at random from a set you submitted a week in advance (twenty topics, so you cannot prepare them all).

**Deliverable:** a recording, plus the panel's assessment.

| Standard | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Structure | a discernible four-tank shape — claim, cases, objections, implications |
| Filler rate | under five per minute |
| Register | consistent, appropriate to the room |
| Questions | answers three questions at the end without losing the thread |
| Native-blind | at least one panelist did not identify you as foreign before you said so |

## B. The story

**3,000 characters** of fiction, in Korean, submitted in advance.

| Standard | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Voice | consistent and audible — the narrator is a person, not a neutral camera |
| Dialogue | carries relationship through endings, not through stage directions |
| Ending | open; the story stops rather than concludes |
| Reader | at least one native reads it to the end, unaided, and can say what it was about |

## C. The defence

A **30-minute** conversation with the panel on your own work — the story, the translation portfolio from Phase 6, and your twenty-year plan.

| Standard | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Fluency | no gap over 1.5 seconds in a three-way exchange |
| Subtext | correctly reads one refusal and one disagreement that are not stated plainly |
| Register | switches correctly when addressed by a senior panelist |
| Content | defends a choice without defensiveness |

## D. The lesson

Teach **60 minutes** to a real learner, observed by one panelist.

| Standard | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Design | backward-designed, outcome stated first |
| Strands | at least three of the four present |
| Correction | ≥ 80% of corrections at rungs 1–3 |
| Change | the learner can do something at the end they could not at the start |

## E. The written test

Two pieces, in Korean, on the day:

1. A **1,200-character** response to an unfamiliar newspaper editorial, arguing with it.
2. A **600-character** explanation of one grammar structure, written for a beginner.

| Standard | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Editorial | a position, two reasons, and one concession handled |
| Grammar | accurate rule, one constraint, one exception, no metalanguage abuse |

## F. The audit

Return to the roadmap's error ledger. Add every error from this phase.

**Deliverable:** a one-page statement of what you are still bad at, written honestly, in Korean and in your first language.

> **This page is not optional and it is not a formality.** A mastery claim without a written account of its limits is a sales pitch. The people who are actually good at languages all know exactly where the holes are.

|---|---|---|
| The hour | 25% | 60 min unprepared, filler < 5/min, structure visible |
| The story | 20% | native reads it to the end |
| The defence | 20% | fluency, subtext, register, under pressure |

## What the roadmap promised, and whether it delivered

The README defined mastery as six things. Here is the accounting.

| Promise | Delivered at | The proof |
|---|---|---|

| Follow a native-speed panel discussion | L5, L15, L17 | 100 clips, 듣기 block H, three-way conversation |

## The honest number

The README said ~2,200–2,600 hours, and 2.5–3 years at 2–3 hours a day. That number does not change at the end of the roadmap, and it was never a promise of speed — it was a promise that the work is finite in one direction and infinite in another.

**Finite:** the structure. Eighteen levels is enough structure. You will not run out of things to learn at 2,600 hours, but you will have built the machine that learns them.

**Infinite:** the rest. The last 5% has no bottom, and L17 is called native-adjacent rather than native for a reason.

## What the roadmap cannot give you

| It cannot | Because |
|---|---|
| make you native | age of onset, and the fact that nativeness is a childhood fact, not an achievement |
| guarantee an accent | prosody is trainable; voice quality mostly is not |
| replace people | every gate from L13 onward requires a native, and every gate in L18 requires a learner |
| survive neglect | the floors in 18.10 are not optional, and the decay is real |

## What to do tomorrow

If you have just read this file from the top and you are not yet at L17, the answer is not this phase. Go back to the README, find your honest level, and start there.

If you are at the end, the answer is the four floors:

1. **30 minutes** of Korean a day that you did not choose for study.
2. **60 minutes** a week of live speech with a native.
3. **10 minutes** a day of retrieval.
4. **One new domain** per year.

That is the whole maintenance regime. It is small enough to survive the weeks when everything else collapses, which is the only property that matters in a plan measured in decades.

## G. Ten failure modes of this phase

| # | Failure | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chasing accent and ignoring subtext | subtext first; accent last |
| 2 | Correcting everything | the rule of two |
| 3 | Teaching grammar you cannot explain | the three-question test |
| 4 | Explaining to a learner who needed to notice | put "notice" before "explain" |
| 5 | Designing forward from a textbook | backward design |
| 6 | All four strands weighted evenly on paper, language-focused in practice | audit your week with a timer |
| 7 | Building a monolithic plan that dies in a bad week | the floors, with fallbacks |
| 8 | Keeping reading and dropping speaking | the output floor is the one that protects the thing that decays fastest |
| 9 | Memorising slang to sound young | understand fifty, use five |
| 10 | Claiming mastery without a written account of the holes | the audit |
